Section 1

What you are actually making

Component 1 is not one thing. It is three things, submitted together, and each one is marked separately.

1 · The product

A film opening or a magazine, in a genre you register early and stick to. This is the thing people see. It is worth 20 of the 50 marks.

2 · The blog

A dated online record of how you made it, written on the day, from the first week of research to the last. It carries 10 marks of its own and quietly protects the rest.

3 · The reflection (CCR)

Four set questions, answered in creative digital formats, not as an essay. Marked twice: once for how well you make it, once for what it says. 20 marks between the two.

You may work in a group of up to four on the production. If you do, every member keeps their own separate blog and labels their own contribution in it. The reflection is always individual, never shared.

The one sentence worth memorising

A strong product with a weak blog cannot reach the top band, because the evidence the top band needs lives in the blog. Cambridge marks what you can show, not what you remember doing.

Section 3

The blog

Cambridge calls it a continuous narrative of the production process. In practice that means one thing: it is written as the work happens, never afterwards.

Three rules that apply to every single post

RuleWhat it meansWhy it matters
Dated on the dayPost it the day the work happened. Do not backfill.A blog written the night before cannot show a process. Moderators can tell.
Contributions labelledIn group work, say what you did.Unlabelled work cannot be credited to you.
No gapsEvery stage appears, in order.Gaps read as incomplete evidence, whatever the product looks like.

The seven posts you are marked on at the mid-point

This is the A2 checklist. It is the same instrument your teacher marks with, so there is no mystery about the standard. Tick as you go, your progress is saved in this browser.

  • ✓1Brief and group registeredWhich brief, which genre, who is in the group and what each person leads on.
  • ✓2Research into similar professional productsReal texts, closely read. Not a summary of the genre in general.
  • ✓3Audience profileA specific audience with evidence. "Everyone aged 15 to 35" is not a profile.
  • ✓4PitchThe idea, stated clearly enough that someone else could picture it.
  • ✓5Planning suiteDetailed enough that someone else could shoot it without asking you questions. See section 4.
  • ✓6Production log openedThe running record of what you actually did, starting now.
  • ✓7Early production evidenceFootage, spreads, stills. Proof that production has genuinely started.
0 of 7 complete
The phrase that decides most marks is "implementation of research". Research that sits in post 2 and never comes back is research nobody implemented. Post 5 and post 7 need to visibly carry what post 2 taught you. A blog can be thorough and still land at 5 or 6 if those posts do not talk to each other.
Check every URL from a signed-out browser. A blog only you can open has not been submitted.
Section 4

The planning suite

This is the single clearest difference between a middle-band portfolio and a top-band one. Not talent. Which planning documents exist.

Cambridge names the planning activities it expects to see. In its own published exemplars, the portfolio that reached the top of the range had these; the one that stopped at the middle was told its planning needed a wider range. Tick what you have actually posted.

  • ✓StoryboardsShot by shot. For magazines, layout roughs and flat plans.
  • ✓Scripting and screenplayDialogue, timings, or cover lines and copy.
  • ✓Recce reportsYou visited the location, photographed it, and noted light, sound, access and problems.
  • ✓CastingWho appears, why they fit, and how you decided.
  • ✓Test shotsEspecially for any effect you plan to use. Cheap to do, easy to evidence, and routinely missing.
  • ✓Risk assessmentWhat could go wrong on the shoot and what you did about it.
0 of 6 complete
Test shots are the one most people skip. If your opening uses any effect at all, a short post showing the test, what failed, and what you changed is some of the easiest evidence in the whole component.
Section 5

The Creative Critical Reflection

Four questions. Answered creatively, in digital formats. This is where the most marks are lost for the least good reason.

Why this section deserves your attention

Cambridge publishes two marked example portfolios. One sits in the middle of the range, one at the very top. Both lose marks in exactly the same place, and it is here. Each used a single application for all four answers, and each moderator wrote the same criticism. For the top portfolio, this was the only strand that fell below the top band.

The syllabus lets you use a different format for every question. Using one tool for all four is a choice, and it is a choice that costs marks for no production reason whatever.

Plan your four formats now

Pick a different tool for each question. If you repeat one, this will tell you.

Question 1
How does your product use or challenge conventions, and how does it represent social groups or issues?
You have already used this format for another question.
Question 2
How does your product engage with audiences, and how would it be distributed as a real media text?
You have already used this format for another question.
Question 3
How did your production skills develop throughout this project?
You have already used this format for another question.
Question 4
How did you integrate technologies, software, hardware and online, in this project?
You have already used this format for another question.
A reflection read off a page as a talking head is answering the questions while failing the format. The reflection is marked twice: once for its own technical and creative skill, once for the quality of the thinking in it. A creative reflection that says nothing loses the second mark. A thoughtful one delivered as a read-aloud essay loses the first.
"All four questions" is the phrase in the top three bands. Answering three brilliantly and one thinly caps that strand at 3 or 4 out of 10, no matter how good the strongest answer is. Even coverage beats one showpiece.
Section 6

How you are marked

Five strands, 10 marks each, 50 total. Each strand is banded separately, which is why a strong product cannot carry a weak reflection. Click a strand to see what each band actually says.

"Knowingly subverts" scores as well as "accurately follows". Breaking a convention on purpose, and being able to explain why in your reflection, sits in the top band alongside observing one perfectly. You are not required to play it safe.
Section 7

Two ways to lose marks you have already earned

Both of these happen to students who did the work. They are evidence problems, not effort problems.

The thin blog

Looks like: few posts, several written in one burst near the deadline, research that never reappears later.

Why it caps you: the top band asks for research drawn on to inform the production. If the blog cannot show that happening, the mark is not available, however good the product is.

Guard: the dating rule. Post on the day, every time.

The misweighted blog

Looks like: plenty of posts, properly dated, but most of the weight sits in preliminary exercises and side material rather than the actual brief.

Why it caps you: this is what happened to one of Cambridge's published exemplars. Well dated, genuine progression, and still capped for carrying too much preliminary material and not enough on the set brief.

Guard: every few weeks, ask how much of your blog is about the thing you are actually being marked on.

The second one is harder to spot, because everything feels fine. You are posting regularly, the dates are honest, the work is real. Count the posts that are about your brief, and compare that number to the total.
