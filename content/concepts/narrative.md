# Narrative

At AS, narrative is mostly about **expectation**: how an opening plants enigmas, establishes roles, and sets a trajectory. It is not a separate key concept. It sits inside [[concepts/media-language|Media Language]] and appears in both the C1 film-opening brief and Component 2 Section A.

## What Cambridge says

Cambridge says little about narrative. The syllabus mentions it twice: media language "develops as genre", and genre is "dynamic and historically relative" (p.14). At A Level it adds "the relationship between postmodernism and narrative" (p.18).

The Scheme of Work mentions narrative twice: openings "set up some **narrative expectations**", and the [[entities/roland-barthes|Roland Barthes]] pointer to "narrative codes".

Cambridge names no narrative-structure model. There is no Todorov, no Propp, no Lévi-Strauss, no three-act structure, no equilibrium/disequilibrium. Barthes' codes are the only narrative apparatus Cambridge offers. The C1 film row names them directly ("Roland Barthes' 5 Narrative Codes"), though the codes themselves are never listed.

Cambridge does include narrative analysis without theory. The editing exercise bank has a narrative-mapping task (linear vs non-linear sequencing, flashbacks, parallel plotlines, cliffhanger placement) and an ellipsis/time-construction task ("How does editing construct narrative clarity or confusion?"). Structure is taught through editing, and the models are taught in class.

## Where it does appear: enigma

The strongest narrative content in Cambridge is the **enigma**, and it comes through production rather than theory.

For C1 story groundwork you "should be able to explain what sort of film this is; who the main character is; what the central conflict is likely to be; **the questions or enigmas that the opening creates** – that is, what information is given? What is withheld? What clues are provided?"

The C1 research prompts ask: "**how does the film establish an enigma from the outset?**"

So enigma is examined twice: as something to analyze in real openings, and as something to construct in your own opening. That double duty makes it the highest-value narrative idea in the AS year.

## The BNDS layer

The Semester 1 scheme teaches a fuller narrative toolkit than Cambridge provides, across two lessons.

**[[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-07-integration-and-the-planning-lock|L07]]** teaches **Todorov's equilibrium**, then **Propp's character functions**, each on a single day. Application turns straight to your own C1 plans: where is the disturbance in your opening. You should be able to map characters by function rather than identity, and name where Propp strains.

**[[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-08-the-viewing-protocol-and-a1|L08]]** puts Propp and Todorov under load on real texts, hunting for where the theory strains, because strain is what a top-band answer discusses. Propp on a trailer, Todorov across an episode. Both days end by logging where the theory failed to fit and why that is analytically interesting.

**Propp and Todorov are BNDS additions.** The syllabus requires "key theories of media studies" (p.13) without naming any, and Cambridge states it does "not prioritise one theory or approach over another" (p.18). Know that you are using a chosen framework, not a prescribed one. Examiners credit the application, not the name.

Since no model is prescribed, the mark cannot come from reciting one. It comes from showing where a model illuminates a text and where it does not.

## What to watch

The syllabus lists "the significance of challenging and/or subverting conventions" (p.14). The Scheme of Work gives no support for it, and the word "subvert" does not appear there. Narrative subversion is a place where the BNDS material originates rather than adapts.
