# Narrative

At AS, narrative is mostly about **expectation**: how an opening plants enigmas, establishes roles, and sets a trajectory. It is not a separate key concept. It sits inside [[concepts/media-language|Media Language]] and surfaces in both the C1 film-opening brief and Component 2 Section A.

## What Cambridge says

The syllabus says little about narrative.

It mentions narrative twice: media language "develops as genre" and genre is "dynamic and historically relative" (p.14), and, at A Level, "the relationship between postmodernism and narrative" (p.18).

The Scheme of Work's Component 2 section mentions narrative only twice in four pages: openings "set up some **narrative expectations**" (SoW p.13), and the [[entities/roland-barthes|Roland Barthes]] pointer to "narrative codes" (SoW p.14).

> [!important] No narrative model is prescribed
> No narrative-structure model is named in any Cambridge document. No Todorov, no Propp, no Lévi-Strauss, no three-act structure, no equilibrium/disequilibrium. Barthes' codes are the only narrative apparatus Cambridge offers. The 2028 C1 film row names them directly ("Roland Barthes' 5 Narrative Codes"), but the codes themselves are still never listed.

The 2028 edition adds narrative *analysis* work without theory. Its editing exercise bank includes a narrative-mapping task (linear vs non-linear sequencing, flashbacks, parallel plotlines, cliffhanger placement) and an ellipsis/time-construction task ("How does editing construct narrative clarity or confusion?"). Structure is taught through editing, and you supply the models.

## Where it does appear: enigma

The strongest narrative content in the Cambridge sources is the **enigma**, and it arrives through production rather than theory.

C1 story groundwork (SoW p.10): you "should be able to explain what sort of film this is; who the main character is; what the central conflict is likely to be; the questions or enigmas that the opening creates – that is, what information is given? What is withheld? What clues are provided?"

And in the C1 research prompts (CWH p.11): "how does the film establish an enigma from the outset?"

So enigma is examined twice over: as a thing to analyze in real openings, and as a thing to construct in your own opening. That double duty makes it the highest-value narrative idea in the AS year.

## The BNDS layer

The Semester 1 scheme teaches a fuller narrative toolkit than Cambridge provides. It sits in one lesson, across both of its days.

**[[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-10-narrative-barthes-and-the-blog-audit|L10]] Day 1, "Todorov, Propp, and the strain log."** **Todorov**'s arc is modeled on one short narrative, tracked on a second, then mapped by each student on a story they know. **Propp** follows: he classifies characters "by what they do, not who they are." Each model is taught with its failure condition attached: Todorov "fails on non-linear narratives, and saying so earns marks," and Propp carries the caveat of its 1928 folk-tale origin. The day opens the **strain log**, a running list titled "Where the theory strains," which is "exam ammunition, not a complaint box." Success criterion: "Map characters by function rather than identity, and name where Propp strains."

**L10 Day 2.** Propp is mapped onto a contemporary trailer, then you log the harder question in writing: "where did Propp fail to fit, and why is that interesting?" You rehearse the exam sentence aloud until it is automatic: "Todorov's model illuminates X, although the extract complicates it by Y." The second half of that sentence is where the marks are.

Propp and Todorov are BNDS additions, not Cambridge requirements. They are defensible: the syllabus requires "key theories of media studies" (p.13) without naming any, and Cambridge states it does "not prioritise one theory or approach over another" (p.18). But know that you are using a chosen framework, not a prescribed one, and examiners will credit the *application*, not the name.

Because no model is prescribed, marks do not come from reciting one. They come from showing where a model illuminates a text and where it does not.

## What to watch

The syllabus has a narrative-adjacent bullet: "the significance of challenging and/or subverting conventions" (p.14). The Scheme of Work gives no teaching support for it, and the word "subvert" does not appear in that document. Narrative subversion is a place where the BNDS material originates rather than adapts.
