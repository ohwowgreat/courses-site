# Power and the Media

Power and the Media is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics (Syllabus 2027 pp.9, 18). It is one of two topics chosen for BNDS, alongside [[concepts/postmodern-media|Postmodern Media]]. The choice will be confirmed in class.

Syllabus framing: "Power and the media considers issues surrounding access to the media, identity, representation, ideology and democracy" (p.18).

The core question: **who gets to speak, who gets spoken for, and whose version of the world wins?**

## What you must study (Syllabus 2027 p.18)

- the relationships between media texts, their producers, distributors and audiences
- the mediation of national, regional, individual and group identities
- the dynamic nature of power relationships within and between audiences and the media
- the power of rhetoric in the media
- the construction and reception of campaigning and marketing messages
- the way access to the media may be limited or controlled, including the democratic distribution of voices

The last five of these are listed as the key areas in the examination.

⚠ Unlike the other two debates, this topic has no "arguments for and against" bullet. You analyze how power operates, rather than argue whether it should.

## Three kinds of power

The topic points to a useful taxonomy:

> "This could be **the power to represent, to speak for a particular group**, or the power to **acquire independent companies in order to benefit from their brand and market share**. It could also be the growing **power of individuals to produce and distribute their own material**, to create their own brands and to make an impact on political discourse via social media."

So there are three kinds: **representational power**, **corporate power**, and **distributed individual power**. A strong essay usually sets at least two of them against each other.

## Dominant ideology

> "although there may now be an enormous quantity of voices in the public sphere, there is still a sense that some voices are more powerful than others, some representations are more potent than others, and that **when media messages are aligned with dominant ideologies they are particularly powerful**."

This connects straight back to [[concepts/representation|Representation]]: the systematic reinforcement of values across many texts is the mechanism by which representation becomes power.

## The named thinker

**[[entities/slavoj-zizek|Slavoj Žižek]]** is the only theorist Cambridge names in this topic. Media ownership is one factor: outlets can be directly influenced by their owners, or can self-censor. But more significant is what Žižek calls the media's **'Standard Operating Procedure'**:

> "its reproduction of ideological 'truths' about which voices count for more than others, its reduction of complex conflicts to spectacle, and the general reinforcing of 'common sense'."

Also consider how much has changed since then in the representation of different ethnicities in media productions, and analyze how and why change has happened in different degrees.

## Counter-power

People can also reject or subvert media messages. One strategy is **détournement**: reworking existing texts so their meaning turns against itself. It is linked to [[entities/guy-debord|Guy Debord]] and the Situationists. Other strategies include producing your own texts or running social media campaigns. Cambridge's example is a Guardian piece on young Brixton activists who recreated film posters with Black leads.

## Activities and resources

One activity fits each syllabus bullet:

- **Media power flow analysis**: producers → distributors → platforms → audiences; gatekeeping and concentration.
- **Mediation of identity**: national, regional, group, and individual.
- **Audience power dynamics**: activism, backlash, and fan campaigns; whether participatory culture increases or commodifies audience power.
- **Rhetorical strategy analysis**: framing, emotive language, and binary oppositions.
- **Campaign analysis**: mode of address, ideological positioning, and oppositional readings.
- **Access mapping**: ownership concentration, economic barriers, **platform algorithms**, and cultural gatekeeping.
- A synoptic debate on the motion "Media power remains concentrated in the hands of institutions rather than audiences."

Resources: Media Reform Coalition, Ofcom media-ownership rules, Pew, and the Reuters Institute Digital News Report.

## Suggested exercises

1. "Find two or three news stories about refugees/asylum seekers. How are they represented? What rhetoric is used? How is the reader positioned in relation to the refugees?"
2. "What kinds of representations of women can you find in advertising? How 'powerful' are they?"

## What the syllabus does not name

The topic names only Žižek. The terms "public sphere," "dominant ideologies," and "common sense" all appear as vocabulary with no named originator. These thinkers and ideas are relevant but not named by the syllabus: **Gramsci**, **hegemony**, **Stuart Hall**, **Habermas**, **Herman & Chomsky**, the **propaganda model**, **Curran & Seaton**, and **Foucault**.

## The specimen answer

A marked high answer and middle answer respond to the question: **"'Now that everyone has a voice in the media, everyone has equal power to influence.' How far do you agree? [15]"**. The high answer scores **12/15** (AO1 7/9, AO2 5/6); the middle answer scores **7/15** (AO1 4/9, AO2 3/6).

The high-band answer uses the very names the topic withholds. It names **Gramsci** and **hegemony**, **Chomsky's *Manufacturing Consent*** and the **Overton Window**, and **Curran & Seaton's *Power Without Responsibility***; the examiner's AO2 breakdown credits "theories of Hegemony and dominant ideology." It also uses **Clay Shirky** for technological determinism, **Evgeny Morozov** against him, **Mark Fisher** on political economy, and **David Buckingham** on social media reproducing offline class advantage.

The two answers separate on structure, not knowledge:

- The middle answer is all illustration: BLM, the Arab Spring, Joe Rogan, and the 2024 US election. The examiner calls the Arab Spring "old" and "quite badly outdated" (15 years), and marks the answer down for describing influencers instead of addressing *everyone*. Not answering the actual question is the failure.
- The high answer argues by counter-evidence. It concedes the democratization case, then dismantles it with shadow-banning research, ownership consolidation, and reach economics. The two marks it misses are for never asking whether a digital voice can actually *change* anything, and for ignoring cost and accessibility barriers.
- The examiner is explicit about case studies: "a collection of associated texts, linked thematically," known well enough to include how they were made, distributed, and consumed. See [[concepts/case-study-method|Case Study Method]].
