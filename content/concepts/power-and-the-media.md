# Power and the Media

Power and the Media is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics (Syllabus 2027 pp.9, 18). It is one of the two debates topics studied at BNDS, alongside [[concepts/postmodern-media|Postmodern Media]]. Your teacher will confirm the choice in class.

Syllabus framing: "Power and the media considers issues surrounding access to the media, identity, representation, ideology and democracy" (p.18).

The core question: **who gets to speak, who gets spoken for, and whose version of the world wins?**

## What candidates must study (Syllabus 2027 p.18)

You study:

- the relationships between media texts, their producers, distributors and audiences
- the mediation of national, regional, individual and group identities
- the dynamic nature of power relationships within and between audiences and the media
- the power of rhetoric in the media
- the construction and reception of campaigning and marketing messages
- the way access to the media may be limited or controlled, including the democratic distribution of voices

The last five are the key areas in the examination (SoW p.25).

> [!note] Interrogate, do not adjudicate
> Unlike the other two debates, this topic has no "arguments for and against" bullet. You analyze how power operates, rather than arguing whether it should. See the past questions below, though: one live paper did ask for a two-sided answer.

## Three kinds of power

Power in the media takes three forms (SoW p.24):

> "This could be **the power to represent, to speak for a particular group**, or the power to **acquire independent companies in order to benefit from their brand and market share**. It could also be the growing **power of individuals to produce and distribute their own material**, to create their own brands and to make an impact on political discourse via social media."

Representational power · corporate power · distributed individual power. Good essays on this topic set at least two of them against each other.

## Dominant ideology

> "although there may now be an enormous quantity of voices in the public sphere, there is still a sense that some voices are more powerful than others, some representations are more potent than others, and that **when media messages are aligned with dominant ideologies they are particularly powerful**." (SoW p.25)

This connects straight to [[concepts/representation|Representation]]: the systematic reinforcement of values across many texts is the mechanism by which representation becomes power.

## The named thinker

**[[entities/slavoj-zizek|Slavoj Žižek]]** is the only theorist Cambridge names in this topic (SoW p.25):

> "Media ownership will be one factor to consider, and there are examples of media outlets being directly influenced, or self-censoring, because of their owners, but more significant will be what **Slavoj Žižek** calls the media's **'Standard Operating Procedure'** – its reproduction of ideological 'truths' about which voices count for more than others, its reduction of complex conflicts to spectacle, and the general reinforcing of 'common sense'."

## Counter-power

Media messages can also be rejected or subverted (SoW p.25):

> "Learners might also consider, however, how media messages may be rejected or subverted, perhaps using strategies of **détournement**, producing their own texts, or mounting social media campaigns."

Cambridge's example is a Guardian piece on young Brixton activists recreating film posters with Black leads. **Détournement** connects to [[entities/guy-debord|Guy Debord]] and the Situationists.

## Activities and resources

Alongside the Žižek passage, also consider: "In the years since the publication of this article, it is now also worth considering how much has changed in terms of increased representation of different ethnicities in media productions - and also analyse how and why degrees of change have occurred."

A structured activity bank runs one exercise per syllabus bullet:

- media power flow analysis (producers → distributors → platforms → audiences; gatekeeping, concentration)
- mediation of national/regional/group/individual identity
- audience power dynamics (activism, backlash, fan campaigns; "whether participatory culture increases or commodifies audience power")
- rhetorical strategy analysis (framing, emotive language, binary oppositions)
- campaign analysis (mode of address, ideological positioning, oppositional readings)
- access mapping (ownership concentration, economic barriers, **platform algorithms**, cultural gatekeeping)
- a synoptic debate on the motion "Media power remains concentrated in the hands of institutions rather than audiences."

Resource links: **Media Reform Coalition, Ofcom media-ownership rules, Pew, Reuters Institute Digital News Report.**

## Suggested exercises

1. "Find two or three news stories about refugees/asylum seekers. How are they represented? What rhetoric is used? How is the reader positioned in relation to the refugees?"
2. "What kinds of representations of women can you find in advertising? How 'powerful' are they?"

## Thinkers Cambridge does not name

The syllabus names only Žižek. These thinkers do not appear, though their ideas surface as bare vocabulary: **Gramsci**, **hegemony**, **Stuart Hall**, **Habermas**, **Herman & Chomsky**, the **propaganda model**, **Curran & Seaton**, **Foucault**. "Public sphere," "dominant ideologies" and "common sense" all appear with no originator credited.

## The specimen answer: every name Cambridge withholds

There is a marked high answer and a marked middle answer to the question **"'Now that everyone has a voice in the media, everyone has equal power to influence.' How far do you agree? [15]"**. High **12/15** (AO1 7/9, AO2 5/6); middle **7/15** (AO1 4/9, AO2 3/6).

The syllabus names nobody, and the top band went to an answer made of names. The high-band answer uses **Gramsci and hegemony**, **Chomsky's *Manufacturing Consent*** and the Overton Window, and **Curran & Seaton's *Power Without Responsibility*** by name. The examiner's AO2 breakdown credits "theories of Hegemony and dominant ideology." It also runs **Clay Shirky** for technological determinism, **Evgeny Morozov** against him, **Mark Fisher** on political economy, and **David Buckingham** on social media reproducing offline class advantage.

The two answers separate on structure, not knowledge:

- **The middle answer is all illustration.** It uses BLM, the Arab Spring, Joe Rogan and the 2024 US election. The examiner calls the Arab Spring "old" and "quite badly outdated" (15 years), and marks the response down for describing influencers instead of addressing *everyone*. Not answering the actual question is the named failure.
- **The high answer argues by counter-evidence.** It concedes the democratization case, then dismantles it with shadow-banning research, ownership consolidation and reach economics. The two marks it misses are for never asking whether a digital voice can actually *change* anything, and for ignoring cost and accessibility barriers.
- **The examiner is explicit about case studies**: "a collection of associated texts, linked thematically," known well enough to include how they were made, distributed and consumed. See [[concepts/case-study-method|Case Study Method]].

## Past questions

Four questions run across the June 2024 and June 2025 papers, always Question 3. They are held unseen for A2 practice; see the [[classes/media-studies/9607-resource-library|Resource Library]]. Paraphrased:

| Paper | Question | Syllabus bullet it instantiates |
|---|---|---|
| Jun 2024 P4/41 | Evaluate the arguments for and against trusting the media | none cleanly; see the note below |
| Jun 2024 P4/42≡43 | Analyse how representations reflect differences in power in society | mediation of identities; dominant ideology |
| Jun 2025 P4/41 | Analyse the power of rhetoric: "not just *what* is said but *how*" | "the power of rhetoric in the media," nearly verbatim |
| Jun 2025 P4/42≡43 | Explain the relationship between the media and democracy | "the democratic distribution of voices" |

> [!note] One past question asked for both sides
> The syllabus bullets contain no "arguments for and against," and the topic is framed as interrogation. But the June 2024 P4/41 paper asked candidates to evaluate the arguments for and against trusting the media, which is a full two-sided essay. Learn the interrogative core, and also rehearse at least one for-and-against framing inside it.

The rhetoric question is the cleanest example in the bank of a syllabus bullet turned into a question sentence. Rehearsing the bullets as questions is literally how the paper is written.
