# Power and the Media

This is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics (Syllabus 2027 pp.9, 18), studied alongside [[concepts/postmodern-media|Postmodern Media]].

Syllabus framing: "Power and the media considers issues surrounding access to the media, identity, representation, ideology and democracy" (p.18).

The core question: **who gets to speak, who gets spoken for, and whose version of the world wins?**

## What you must study (Syllabus 2027 p.18)

- the relationships between media texts, their producers, distributors and audiences
- the mediation of national, regional, individual and group identities
- the dynamic nature of power relationships within and between audiences and the media
- the power of rhetoric in the media
- the construction and reception of campaigning and marketing messages
- the way access to the media may be limited or controlled, including the democratic distribution of voices

The last five are named as key areas in the examination.

> [!note] How to approach this topic
> Unlike the other two debates, this topic has **no "arguments for and against."** It is framed as interrogation, not adjudication: you analyze how power operates, rather than arguing whether it should.

## Three kinds of power

> "This could be **the power to represent, to speak for a particular group**, or the power to **acquire independent companies in order to benefit from their brand and market share**. It could also be the growing **power of individuals to produce and distribute their own material**, to create their own brands and to make an impact on political discourse via social media."

Representational power · corporate power · distributed individual power. Most good essays set at least two of them against each other.

## Dominant ideology

> "although there may now be an enormous quantity of voices in the public sphere, there is still a sense that some voices are more powerful than others, some representations are more potent than others, and that **when media messages are aligned with dominant ideologies they are particularly powerful**."

This connects straight back to [[concepts/representation|Representation]] — the systematic reinforcement of values across many texts is the mechanism by which representation becomes power.

## The named thinker

**[[entities/slavoj-zizek|Slavoj Žižek]]** is the only theorist Cambridge names here:

> "Media ownership will be one factor to consider, and there are examples of media outlets being directly influenced, or self-censoring, because of their owners, but more significant will be what **Slavoj Žižek** calls the media's **'Standard Operating Procedure'** – its reproduction of ideological 'truths' about which voices count for more than others, its reduction of complex conflicts to spectacle, and the general reinforcing of 'common sense'."

Also worth considering: how much has changed in terms of increased representation of different ethnicities in media productions since that article, and how and why degrees of change have occurred.

## Counter-power

> "Learners might also consider, however, how media messages may be rejected or subverted, perhaps using strategies of **détournement**, producing their own texts, or mounting social media campaigns."

One example is a Guardian piece on young Brixton activists recreating film posters with Black leads. Détournement connects to [[entities/guy-debord|Guy Debord]] and the Situationists.

## Activities

One activity per syllabus area:

- **Media power flow analysis** — producers → distributors → platforms → audiences; gatekeeping, concentration
- **Mediation of identity** — national/regional/group/individual
- **Audience power dynamics** — activism, backlash, fan campaigns; whether participatory culture increases or commodifies audience power
- **Rhetorical strategy analysis** — framing, emotive language, binary oppositions
- **Campaign analysis** — mode of address, ideological positioning, oppositional readings
- **Access mapping** — ownership concentration, economic barriers, platform algorithms, cultural gatekeeping
- **Synoptic debate** on the motion "Media power remains concentrated in the hands of institutions rather than audiences."

Additional exercises:

1. Find two or three news stories about refugees/asylum seekers. How are they represented? What rhetoric is used? How is the reader positioned in relation to the refugees?
2. What kinds of representations of women can you find in advertising? How "powerful" are they?

## Who Cambridge names — and doesn't

Žižek is the sole named thinker in the topic. Not named: Gramsci, hegemony, Stuart Hall, Habermas, Herman & Chomsky, the propaganda model, Curran & Seaton, Foucault. "Public sphere," "dominant ideologies" and "common sense" all appear as bare vocabulary with no originator credited.

## The specimen answer

There is a marked high and middle answer to the question:

**"'Now that everyone has a voice in the media, everyone has equal power to influence.' How far do you agree?" [15]**

High **12/15** (AO1 7/9, AO2 5/6); middle **7/15** (AO1 4/9, AO2 3/6).

Even though Cambridge names only Žižek, the top-band answer is built on named theory: **Gramsci and hegemony**, **Chomsky's *Manufacturing Consent*** and the Overton Window, and **Curran & Seaton's *Power Without Responsibility*** — and the examiner's AO2 breakdown credits "theories of Hegemony and dominant ideology." It also uses **Clay Shirky** for technological determinism, **Evgeny Morozov** against him, **Mark Fisher** on political economy, and **David Buckingham** on social media reproducing offline class advantage.

The two answers separate on structure, not knowledge:

- **The middle answer is all illustration.** BLM, the Arab Spring, Joe Rogan and the 2024 US election — the examiner calls the Arab Spring "old" and "quite badly outdated" (15 years), and marks it down for describing influencers instead of addressing *everyone*. Not answering the actual question is the named failure.
- **The high answer argues by counter-evidence.** It concedes the democratization case, then dismantles it with shadow-banning research, ownership consolidation and reach economics. The two marks it misses are for never asking whether a digital voice can actually *change* anything, and for ignoring cost and accessibility barriers.
- **The examiner is explicit about case studies:** "a collection of associated texts, linked thematically," known well enough to include how they were made, distributed and consumed. See [[concepts/case-study-method|Case Study Method]].
