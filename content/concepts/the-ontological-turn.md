# The Ontological Turn

**The pivot from asking *how well can we know things* to asking *what kind of things are there to know*.**

The **epistemological question** assumes a stable reality and asks how reliable our access to it is. The **ontological question** suspends that assumption.

## The classroom version

Think of the tree falling in the forest.

> Those who say no sound are not claiming that our hearing is unreliable. They are claiming that **sound is a relation rather than a property.**

That distinction is the whole idea. It is why [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w03-can-measurement-reach-the-thing-itself|W03]] closes on the question without discussing it and [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w04-is-there-a-reality-behind-appearances|W04]] opens by polling it. You arrive having taken a side without knowing which question you answered.

## Why the course needs it

This is the move that makes the second half of the course possible. Once reality is allowed to be relational, questions about history, the body, machines, and collective agreement all become live in the same way.

Each later unit is the same turn applied to a new domain:

| Unit | The relational claim |
|---|---|
| [[classes/oxbridge/unit-plans/oxbridge-unit-3-frameworks-and-history\|U3]] | What counts as a disease or a species is historical, not fixed |
| [[classes/oxbridge/unit-plans/oxbridge-unit-4-the-body-and-the-machine\|U4]] | Agency and understanding may be separable from being alive |
| [[classes/oxbridge/unit-plans/oxbridge-unit-6-collective-reality\|U6]] | Money, nations and debts are real by a relation, not a property |

## Telling the two apart

W04 follows up with a test of whether the turn has actually happened:

> Is this an argument about the world or an argument about a word? **How would you tell the difference?**

If you cannot separate the two, you have made a **semantic point** and mistaken it for an ontological one. That is the most common failure mode of this concept, so name it when you see it.
