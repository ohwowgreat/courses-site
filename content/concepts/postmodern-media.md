# Postmodern Media

This is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics (Syllabus 2027 pp.9, 18). It is one of the two studied at BNDS, alongside [[concepts/power-and-the-media|Power and the Media]]. The final choice will be confirmed in class.

The core question: **is the distinction between reality and representation collapsing, and does it matter?**

## The syllabus framing (p.18)

> "Postmodernism can be characterised by modes such as pastiche, parody, intertextuality, self-referentiality and a conscious engagement with other texts. **We do not prioritise one theory or approach over another, and candidates are encouraged to explore a range of approaches and perspectives, across a range of media forms.**"

## What you must study (Syllabus 2027 p.18)

- the different versions of postmodernism (historical period, style, theoretical approach)
- the arguments for and against understanding some forms of media as postmodern
- the ways postmodern media texts can challenge traditional relationships between texts and audiences
- the relationship between postmodernism and popular culture
- the ways media audiences and industries operate differently in a postmodern world
- the relationship between postmodernism and narrative

Examination questions focus on the last five of these (SoW p.24).

The bullet on **"arguments for and against understanding some forms of media as postmodern"** matters. The examinable position includes skepticism. You may argue that postmodernism is an unhelpful lens, provided you argue it with evidence.

## The concept is unstable

> "Postmodernism is notoriously tricky to pin down… it can refer to **a general condition of contemporary life** in which representations, reality, surfaces and styles are chaotically intermingled, or it can refer to **specific modes of communication** which are arch, self-referential and knowing (irony is often referred to as exemplary of the postmodern mode)." (SoW p.23)

That two-way split, condition versus textual mode, is a good essay structure in itself.

## The two named thinkers

**[[entities/guy-debord|Guy Debord]]** (SoW p.23): *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967), "a critique of media-saturated capitalist culture… still relevant in diagnosing aspects of the postmodern condition."

**[[entities/fredric-jameson|Fredric Jameson]]** (SoW pp.23–24) "diagnoses the postmodern condition as characterised by":

- "An ahistorical mode (lacking a sense of the past – history becomes another set of signifiers which can be played with)."
- "A lack of distinction between 'high' and 'low' culture."
- "Surfaces – increasingly there is no time for depth – politics becomes soundbites, protests become slogans, the world is a collection of images."
- "Affectlessness – the predominant mode of irony and 'knowingness' leaves little room for real emotion, but short, sharp intense emotional responses are elicited through surface representations of things of which people have no first-hand experience, e.g.: Brexit, the death of Diana, refugees."
- "Technologies of reproduction and recirculation, e.g.: all social media."

Each of these five converts directly into an essay paragraph with a contemporary example.

## Ways to explore the topic

- **Conceptual framework comparison**: postmodernism as historical period versus stylistic mode versus theoretical approach, tabulated with media examples.
- **Textual feature analysis**: identify pastiche, parody, intertextuality, self-referentiality, and genre hybridity in one chosen text.
- **Structured critical debate**: "Contemporary media is fundamentally postmodern" versus "Postmodernism is an overused or outdated concept."
- **Narrative deconstruction**: linear versus postmodern structure: non-linearity, fragmentation, ambiguity, open endings.
- **Text/audience relationships**: participatory culture, fan remixing, meme circulation, and whether this increases or commodifies audience power.
- **Intertextuality mapping**: a network diagram of references, borrowings, and parodies, and whether intertextuality "supports commercial strategies."
- **Essay planning workshop**: command word, analytical strands, counterarguments.

Resource links include the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy and a **Jean Baudrillard** pointer (Stanford Encyclopedia entry). This is a link, not an attribution.

## Suggested activities

- **Foraging exercise**: gather online definitions and discussions of postmodernism on your own, then test and secure them together in class. Cambridge notes "there are plenty of definitions and discussions online."
- Test **pastiche, intertextuality and parody** against real media texts as characteristics emerge.
- Identify "what sorts of repertoires of knowledge and experience are necessary in order to make sense of certain media texts, and how they might function '**dialogically**' – that is, say one thing, but invoke other meanings through referencing or quoting from something else."
- Identify "ways in which belief in certain kinds of reality can be less important than belief in media representations of that reality."
- Watch a TV news story and ask what features it has of "The Society of the Spectacle."
- Read up on the difference between pastiche and parody, then find contemporary examples of each.

You already meet postmodern media every day: memes, remixes, and franchise self-reference. Start from your own examples, then add the names.

## Technique note

> "As always, **anchoring the arguments in specific texts or phenomena is key** to demonstrating a sophisticated understanding of the concept and how it might be useful in making sense of contemporary media." (SoW p.24)

## Terms the syllabus does not name

The syllabus and Scheme of Work never name: **hyperreality, simulacra, Lyotard, metanarratives, Bakhtin, Hutcheon, Strinati**. "Dialogically" is used without crediting Bakhtin, and intertextuality is never attributed. **Baudrillard** appears only as a resource link, not as an attributed theory. If a lesson teaches Baudrillard's concepts, that is a choice the syllabus's pluralism (p.18) allows.

## The live questions

There are four past questions on this topic, always Question 2. They are held unseen for A2 practice; see the [[classes/media-studies/9607-resource-library|Resource Library]]. Paraphrased:

| Paper | Question | What it reaches for |
|---|---|---|
| Jun 2024 P4/41 | "The postmodern text requires a media-literate audience": to what extent | the "texts and audiences" bullet; repertoires of knowledge, dialogic reading |
| Jun 2024 P4/42≡43 | Explain why certain texts can be called postmodern | the textual modes: pastiche, parody, intertextuality, self-reference |
| Jun 2025 P4/41 | Explain the relationship between the postmodern text and other media texts | the syllabus's "conscious engagement with other texts" phrase turned into a question |
| Jun 2025 P4/42≡43 | "Postmodernism is simply parody and pastiche": to what extent | the condition-versus-mode split; Jameson's five symptoms are the rebuttal |

Two notes on answering. The "simply parody and pastiche" statement is answerable almost directly from the two-way split (condition versus textual mode): agree about the mode, then widen to the condition. And every question rewards the skepticism position the syllabus allows; if you can argue that postmodernism is overused, you have material for all four.
