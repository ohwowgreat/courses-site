# Representation

**Key Concept 2.** "How the media construct the social world, the portrayal of ideas, individuals and groups" (Syllabus 2027 p.14).

This is the concept that turns description into argument. Component 2 Section A does not ask you to list techniques. It asks how the extract *constructs* a person, group, event, or place, and to what effect.

## The chain

The syllabus's own logic, compressed from the p.14 bullets:

**reality → selection and combination → mediation → a version**

Producers choose what is shown and how it is combined. Repeated choices harden into typicality. Typicality carries values. Values reinforced across many texts become ideology, and ideology positions audiences.

The Scheme of Work's glossary makes the hardening explicit (SoW p.27):

> "The processes (and end results) by which reality is subject to selection, exaggeration and repetition, so that certain representations (often of subordinated groups and associated places) become typical and taken for granted."

## What the syllabus requires

You study (Syllabus 2027 p.14):

- the ways in which events, issues, individuals and social groups are represented through processes of selection and combination
- the ways in which, through representation, versions of reality are mediated
- the processes which lead media producers to make choices about how to represent events, issues, individuals and social groups
- the effect of social and cultural context on representations
- how and why particular social groups, in a national and global context, may be under-represented or misrepresented
- how representations convey values, attitudes and beliefs about the world and how these may be systematically reinforced across a wide range of media
- how audiences respond to and interpret representations in the media
- the effect of historical context on representations
- how representations may invoke discourses and ideologies, and position audiences
- how interpretations of representations reflect social, cultural and historical circumstances

This is the longest content list of the four key concepts. It is the only one that names **under-representation** and **misrepresentation** as objects of study in their own right.

Many bullets are about context and change: social, cultural, historical, national, and global. Representation is not a fixed set of stereotypes. It shifts with who is making, when, and for whom. That is why theories of the gaze are tested across decades, not as settled fact.

## How it enters the exam

Component 2 Section A fuses representation with [[concepts/technical-elements|Technical Elements]]. Candidates "discuss… how the following technical elements create specific representations of individuals/groups/events/places, and help to articulate specific messages and values that have social significance" (Syllabus 2027 p.16).

So the exam question is always a version of this: **how is this person, group, or place constructed, and to what effect?** A script that names techniques but never reaches representation has answered half the question.

The reassembly stage of [[concepts/the-viewing-protocol|The Viewing Protocol]] is where you practice this handoff: "How are particular representations of individuals, groups, events and places constructed through the technical elements identified?" (SoW p.14).

## Ways to practice

- **Compare the coverage.** Find two or three news stories about refugees or asylum seekers. How are they represented? What rhetoric is used? How is the reader positioned in relation to the refugees? Look at what is selected, what is left out, and where you are placed. This scales up to the [[concepts/power-and-the-media|Power and the Media]] debate.
- **Magazine representation grid.** To what extent does the image conform to conventional ways of portraying men, women, and celebrities? How are ethnic, cultural, and subcultural dimensions emphasized or not?
- **Advertising and power.** What kinds of representations of women can you find in advertising? How "powerful" are they?
- **Stills annotation.** Annotate frames with technical terms plus the representation each choice constructs.
- **Genre and representation.** Compare two drama extracts: how does mise-en-scène reinforce genre conventions? Or re-design one scene across social realism, noir crime, teen melodrama, and sci-fi dystopia.

## Theorists and axes of representation

The **axes of representation** are the demographic groups you analyze in Section A: **class, age, gender, ethnicity**. Some texts use stereotypical representations of these groups. Other texts consciously challenge stereotypes.

Run each technical element through these axes:

- Camera: Who is shot from a high or low angle? Who gets the close-ups?
- Sound: How does vocal performance construct representation of class, age, gender, or ethnicity?
- Mise-en-scène: Who occupies the foreground? Who is marginalized spatially?

Theorists of representation you may study include **Hall**, **Van Zoonen**, **bell hooks**, **Laura Mulvey**, [[entities/paul-gilroy]], and [[entities/manuel-alvarado]]. [[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-12-the-gaze|L12]] teaches Van Zoonen, hooks, and Mulvey. Key ideas from this work include **gender as performance**, the **three looks**, and the **oppositional gaze**.

A **stereotype** is a representation that becomes typical and taken for granted through selection, exaggeration, and repetition. Texts may use stereotypes or work to challenge them.
