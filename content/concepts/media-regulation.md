# Media Regulation

Media Regulation is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics; you must study at least two of the three. It is examined in Component 4 Section A as one 15-mark essay, "referring to one or more media forms."

Syllabus framing: "Media regulation considers issues of morality and decency, accuracy of content and media ownership."

The core question: **who should control what media can say and show, and does control actually work?**

## What you must study

- the nature of contemporary and historical media regulation
- the arguments for and against specific forms of media regulation
- the effectiveness of regulatory practices, including the ability to impose or recommend sanctions
- the wider social issues relating to media regulation
- the roles of different regulatory bodies in relation to ownership, morality and accuracy
- the legal and ethical frameworks within which media producers and distributors must operate

Note the second point: **"arguments for and against."** This topic is assessed as a two-sided argument, not a description of a regulatory system. If you explain how regulation works but never argue about whether it should exist, you are answering the wrong question.

## Sub-topics

Regulation can include: taste and decency · illegality and incitement to crime · ownership and pluralism · intellectual property and piracy · public service and responsibilities to the nation · misinformation.

The internet complicates all of them: "The debates about internet regulation are particularly pertinent and action is problematic because of its lack of central organisation and the fact that it is an international medium, so does not fall under a particular jurisdiction."

## Activities you may work through

- **Regulatory authority comparison** — two regulatory organisations compared for legal authority, enforcement powers, sanctions, criticisms, and challenges in the digital era (you choose which two)
- **Regulation in context** — trigger / technological context / reactive-or-proactive / intended and unintended outcomes for key regulatory developments
- **Regulatory sanctions case study** — breach, speed of response, severity, impact, public trust
- **Ideological debate** — assigned positions (free market, public service, critical theory) arguing "The media should be strictly regulated to protect society"
- **Regulatory gap mapping** — streaming, social media, influencer marketing, UGC; jurisdiction issues and "platform vs publisher responsibility"
- **Global regulation comparison** — "Is national regulation still effective in a globalised media industry?"
- **Question planning workshop** — command words, argument strands, counter-argument, peer-assessed against AO1/AO2

## How this topic is approached at BNDS

> [!important] Sensitivity of the topic
> "It is preferable to study regulation in your country/region. However, **this may not always be possible because of the sensitivity of the subject.** If this is the case, and you still wish to explore this topic, it is suggested that you research **the UK model**. There is plenty of material available on the Internet to facilitate this approach. **Alternatively, an approach to regulation could be taken which focuses on intellectual property, ownership, piracy or misinformation – issues of morality are only one dimension.**"

The historical timeline activity carries the same caution about handling material sensitively according to the political situation of the region.

Three routes are therefore possible: study your own region; substitute the UK model; or reframe onto IP, ownership, piracy and misinformation rather than morality and press freedom. At BNDS the workable path is the third route, or the UK model studied comparatively. Related debates topics are [[concepts/postmodern-media|Postmodern Media]] and [[concepts/power-and-the-media|Power and the Media]].

## Suggested teaching activities

- **Opening discussion:** "How is Media regulated where you live? / Has Media always been regulated in this way? / What effect does Media regulation have on consumption? / Are there different ways of regulating the Media? / How do other countries/regions regulate their Media?"
- **Historical timeline:** groups take different decades of the 20th/21st centuries and different media (TV, Film, Print, Radio, Online), research developments in regulation during their period, and present; findings combine into a visual timeline of the historical context of regulation.
- **Comparative research:** regulation in other countries, "especially the UK and the USA," compared with your own.
- **Independent case studies**, then shared in preparation for responding to an unseen question about media regulation.

## No named bodies or theorists in the syllabus

The syllabus names no theorist, no theory, and not a single regulatory body for this topic itself. Activities say "learners select two regulatory organisations" and leave the selection open. Ofcom appears only as a resource link elsewhere ("Ofcom – Media Ownership Rules" under the Power topic; "Ofcom – Online Nation Report" under Changing media environments). Any regulator, framework or thinker in your lessons is a choice made at this centre.

## What the specimen answer shows

There is a marked high and middle answer to **"Evaluate the reasons for and against stricter media regulation" [15]** — the exact Section A regulation question shape. The high answer scores **13/15** (AO1 8/9, AO2 5/6); the middle answer **8/15** (AO1 5/9, AO2 3/6).

Although the syllabus names nobody, the high-band answer is built on named theory throughout — **Stanley Cohen's moral panic** worked through all five stages, **Martin Barker** on the *Ill Effects* lineage of censorship, **David Buckingham**, **Sonia Livingstone** critiquing **Jonathan Haidt**, and **Gramsci** on spontaneous consent. The examiner credits exactly this: the response "synthesises theory very well and goes above and beyond the usual theory references." The middle answer reaches for Uses and Gratifications and the hypodermic model as an add-on, and is marked down for that bolted-on quality.

So the reading of theory for this topic: named thinkers are not prescribed, but the top band is hard to reach without them. Your theory choices are the difference between the middle and top bands.

Two more things the examiner asks for that the syllabus does not state outright:

- **Use contemporary regulatory events, not the canon.** The high answer runs on the UK Online Safety Act, age verification and VPN circumvention, platform moderation. The common-error list opens with "candidates should be focused on contemporary material and examples." This is consistent with the [[concepts/case-study-method|five-year rule]].
- **Argue both sides.** The high answer loses its two marks for having *no* paragraph making the case *for* stricter regulation — the examiner names freedom of speech versus the right not to be harmed as the missing move.

> [!note] Use the structure, substitute the examples
> The specimen's examples are UK-centric and politically live (Online Safety Act, Molly Russell, TikTok/Oracle, a 2025 political killing). Given the sensitivity advice above, follow the *structure* of the high answer with substituted regional examples rather than reusing its examples whole.
