# Media Regulation

Media Regulation is one of the three **A Level Media debates** topics. You must study at least two of the three (Syllabus 2027 pp.9, 18). It is examined in Component 4 Section A as one 15-mark essay, "referring to one or more media forms" (p.26).

Syllabus framing: "Media regulation considers issues of morality and decency, accuracy of content and media ownership" (p.18).

The core question: **who should control what media can say and show, and does control actually work?**

## What you must study (Syllabus 2027 p.18)

- the nature of contemporary and historical media regulation
- the arguments for and against specific forms of media regulation
- the effectiveness of regulatory practices, including the ability to impose or recommend sanctions
- the wider social issues relating to media regulation
- the roles of different regulatory bodies in relation to ownership, morality and accuracy
- the legal and ethical frameworks within which media producers and distributors must operate

The last five are required by the syllabus, not just suggested.

Note the second bullet: **"arguments for and against."** This topic is assessed as a two-sided argument, not a description of a regulatory system. If you explain how regulation works but never argue about whether it should exist, you are answering the wrong question.

## Sub-topics (p.23)

"Regulation could include: taste and decency · illegality and incitement to crime · ownership and pluralism · intellectual property and piracy · public service and responsibilities to the nation."

The internet complicates all of them: "The debates about internet regulation are particularly pertinent and action is problematic because of its lack of central organisation and the fact that it is an international medium, so does not fall under a particular jurisdiction" (p.23).

## Extra focus and activities

Regulation can also focus on **misinformation**, alongside intellectual property, ownership and piracy. These activities support the topic:

- **Regulatory authority comparison:** compare two regulatory organizations for legal authority, enforcement powers, sanctions, criticisms, and "challenges in the digital era." You choose which two.
- **Regulation in context:** for key regulatory developments, map the trigger, the technological context, whether the response was reactive or proactive, and the intended and unintended outcomes.
- **Regulatory sanctions case study:** a breach, speed of response, severity, impact, and public trust.
- **Ideological debate:** assigned positions (free market, public service, critical theory) argue "The media should be strictly regulated to protect society."
- **Regulatory gap mapping:** streaming, social media, influencer marketing, UGC; jurisdiction issues and "platform vs publisher responsibility."
- **Global regulation comparison:** "Is national regulation still effective in a globalised media industry?"
- **Question planning workshop:** command words, argument strands, counter-argument, peer-assessed against AO1/AO2.

## ⚠ Cambridge's sensitivity advice

This is the only topic in the Scheme of Work that carries a handling caveat.

> "It is preferable to study regulation in your country/region. However, **this may not always be possible because of the sensitivity of the subject.** If this is the case, and you still wish to explore this topic, it is suggested that you encourage learners to research **the UK model**. There is plenty of material available on the Internet to facilitate this approach. **Alternatively, an approach to regulation could be taken which focuses on intellectual property, ownership, piracy or misinformation – issues of morality are only one dimension.**" (p.22)

And on the historical timeline activity: "(Once again, this needs to be handled sensitively according to the political situation of the country/region where the material is being delivered.)" (p.22)

Cambridge allows three routes: study your own region (preferred); substitute the UK model; or reframe onto intellectual property, ownership, piracy and misinformation rather than morality and press freedom.

At BNDS, the workable path is the third route, or the UK model studied comparatively. This is the alternative Cambridge names in its own document, not an evasion of the syllabus. The current pairing at BNDS is [[concepts/postmodern-media|Postmodern Media]] and [[concepts/power-and-the-media|Power and the Media]], with regulation as a possible swap. This will be confirmed in class.

## Suggested activities (pp.22–23)

- **Opening discussion:** "How is Media regulated where you live? / Has Media always been regulated in this way? / What effect does Media regulation have on consumption? / Are there different ways of regulating the Media? / How do other countries/regions regulate their Media?"
- **Historical timeline:** groups take different decades of the 20th/21st centuries and different media (TV, Film, Print, Radio, Online), research the regulation developments in their period, and present. Findings combine into "a visual timeline of the historical context of regulation."
- **Comparative research:** regulation in other countries, "especially the UK and the USA," compared with your own.
- **Independent case studies**, then shared "in preparation for responding to an unseen question about media regulation."

## ⚠ No prescribed theorists or bodies

The syllabus and Schemes of Work name no theorist, no theory, and no regulatory body for this topic. There is no BBFC, no IPSO, no NRTA. The activities say you select two regulatory organizations and leave the choice open. The only regulator that appears is Ofcom, as a resource link elsewhere in the Scheme of Work ("Ofcom – Media Ownership Rules" under the Power topic; "Ofcom – Online Nation Report" under Changing media environments). Any regulator, framework, or thinker you use for this topic is your choice, not a requirement.

## The specimen answer

A marked high answer and middle answer respond to **"Evaluate the reasons for and against stricter media regulation" [15]**, the exact Section A regulation question shape. The high answer scores **13/15** (AO1 8/9, AO2 5/6); the middle answer scores **8/15** (AO1 5/9, AO2 3/6).

The syllabus names nobody, but the high-band answer is built on named theory throughout: **Stanley Cohen's moral panic** worked through all five stages, **Martin Barker** on the *Ill Effects* lineage of censorship, **David Buckingham**, **Sonia Livingstone** critiquing **Jonathan Haidt**, and **Gramsci** on spontaneous consent. The examiner credits exactly this: the response "synthesises theory very well and goes above and beyond the usual theory references." The middle answer reaches for Uses and Gratifications and the hypodermic model as an add-on, and is marked down for that bolted-on quality.

So Cambridge does not prescribe thinkers, but the standard it rewards is hard to reach without them. Your named-theory choices are the difference between the middle and top bands.

Two more things the examiner asks for that the syllabus does not state:

- **Contemporary regulatory events, not the canon.** The high answer runs on the UK Online Safety Act, age verification and VPN circumvention, and platform moderation. The common-error list opens with "candidates should be focused on contemporary material and examples." This matches the [[concepts/case-study-method|five-year rule]].
- **Argue both sides.** The high answer loses its two marks for having no paragraph making the case *for* stricter regulation. The examiner names freedom of speech versus the right not to be harmed as the missing move. Build this into your answer.

⚠ The specimen's examples are UK-centric and politically live (Online Safety Act, Molly Russell, TikTok/Oracle, a 2025 political killing). Given Cambridge's sensitivity advice above, use the *structure* of the high answer with substituted regional examples rather than handing over the booklet whole.

## The live questions

The 2024 and 2025 papers give this topic four questions, always Question 1 on the paper, held unseen for A2 practice (see the [[classes/media-studies/9607-resource-library|Resource Library]]). Paraphrased:

| Paper | Question | Syllabus bullet it instantiates |
|---|---|---|
| Jun 2024 P4/41 | Explain why media regulation is necessary | the *for* side of "arguments for and against" |
| Jun 2024 P4/42≡43 | "Regulation is just censorship by another name": to what extent | "arguments for and against" plus "wider social issues" |
| Jun 2025 P4/41 | "Without media regulation there would be chaos": to what extent | "arguments for and against" again |
| Jun 2025 P4/42≡43 | Assess the effectiveness of the sanctions imposed by media regulators | "effectiveness of regulatory practices, including the ability to impose or recommend sanctions" |

Three of the four are the for-and-against bullet in different clothes: this topic is assessed as a two-sided argument. The fourth is the sanctions bullet nearly word for word, and the "Regulatory sanctions case study" activity rehearses it. Until the first series of the new specification (June 2028) produces an examiner report, these four are the only live questions the topic has.
