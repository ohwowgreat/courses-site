## What you will be able to do

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Write in one sentence what your collage is about, not what is in it
2. Produce two different composition drafts of the same main idea
3. Circle one draft and give the reason it argues the idea more strongly

## Goal

This lesson turns your experiments into a work. The question of the day: *what is your collage about?* Not "what is in it" — what it **says**. Every technique from L10–11 serves one **main idea**, which you write in one sentence before you start composing. Then you make two different drafts of the same idea, because the first arrangement is rarely the strongest.

## How it runs

- **Meaning (15 min)** — **Romare Bearden**: *The Block*, *Three Folk Musicians* — collage as memory and neighborhood, cut paper standing in for a whole world. Earlier examples make the same point: a Dutch **vanitas** heaps books, globes and jewels together to say *none of this lasts* — objects juxtaposed into a sentence, 1662. Composition is how the sentence gets its grammar.

- **Main idea (10 min)** — write one sentence in your sketchbook: "My collage is about … " Concrete beats abstract ("my grandmother's kitchen" beats "memory"). An accessible fallback format is the **silhouette self-portrait**: your profile filled with what fills your head. State your idea aloud at your table; the teacher initials each.
- **Studio: two drafts (45 min)** — make two dry-fit compositions of the same idea on separate boards, deliberately different (one crowded / one sparse, one symmetrical / one tilted). No glue today. Photograph both. Then circle one and write the reason.
- **CS3 + close (20 min)** — **CS3 is collected**: the experiment set (juxtaposition, scale, layering, text-and-image + home experiments), checked for range and honest annotation. You will then learn about the final collage, made in class W14–W15 from your circled draft, marked on **juxtaposition, layering, composition, meaning**. The required format will be confirmed in class. Post to MB.
