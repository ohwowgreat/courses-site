## Overview

This lesson covers sound, the second of the four technical elements of media language. You learn the sound lexicon and how sound shapes what an audience thinks it sees. You produce **[[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|CS4]]**, a 30-second soundscape for your own C1 opening. You record it, post it dated to the blog, and write a rationale that names every diegetic and non-diegetic choice. CS4 is due Fri 2026-09-18. It feeds your C1 production, [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]], [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a4-section-a-authentic-format|A4]], and Component 2 Section A, and it is Component 1 practice evidence. Sound also carries every C3 video task, so the work here returns later.

## The ideas

### Diegetic and non-diegetic sound

**Diegetic** sound exists in the world of the text: the characters could hear it. **Non-diegetic** sound is added for the audience only, such as a score. **Synchronous** sound matches the image on screen; **asynchronous** sound does not line up with what you see. A borderline case is worth defending in either direction, as long as you can say why.

### The sound lexicon

- **Sound bridge**: audio that crosses a cut, carrying before or after the picture.
- **Sound motif**: a sound that recurs and gains meaning.
- **Sound perspective**: how near or far a sound seems.
- **Ambient sound** (or sound bed): the background sound of a place.
- **Dialogue**, **voiceover**, and **direct address**: speech aimed at other characters, at the audience over the action, or straight to the audience.
- **Sound mixing**: how the separate sounds are balanced together.
- **Soundtrack**: score, incidental music, themes, and **stings**, a short sharp musical hit.

Extension vocabulary, worth having and rarely seen in scripts: harmony, dissonance, discordance, rhythm, crescendo, pitch, and timbre.

### What sound proves

Play a sequence with the screen black and you can reconstruct a whole scene from audio alone. The gap between what you imagine and what the picture actually shows is the point: it shows how much work sound was doing while you watched the images.

### Music and genre

Take one sequence, change only the non-diegetic music bed, and the genre flips. Non-diegetic music alone can move a text between genres. Ask which choices the audience made and which were made for them.

### Foley

**Foley** is sound recorded to stand in for real sound: footsteps, a door, cloth, a punch, liquid poured. The rule from professional practice is that it does not have to be the real thing, it has to *sound* like the real thing. Two habits matter for every take: headphones on, and the mic as close as the frame allows. Recording near an open door and playing it back on a speaker shows what an uncontrolled space costs.

## Day by day

- **Wed 09-16 (double).** Hear a sequence twice with the screen black, reconstruct it, then watch it and compare. Land the sound lexicon on moments from that sequence. Watch a recut trailer with two different music beds to see the genre flip. Record foley at stations on phones, keeping your best three takes. CS4 is briefed. Homework: collect three sounds from your own environment, recorded on a phone, before Friday.
- **Fri 09-18.** Lexicon Bingo retrieval round from the sound lexicon, no notes. Build your **CS4** soundscape from your own recordings, your foley takes, and one non-diegetic element. It must include at least one clearly diegetic sound, one clearly non-diegetic sound, one sound with a near or far perspective, and one that carries across an implied cut as a bridge. Upload it to the blog, dated, with a rationale that names each choice by its term and says what it should do to the listener. Two soundscapes are then played blind, and the room says what the opening looks like.

## Review

Check you can:

- Distinguish **diegetic** from **non-diegetic** sound and defend a borderline case.
- Use the sound lexicon accurately: **sound bridge**, **sound motif**, **sound perspective**, **ambient sound**, score, **sting**, voiceover, and **synchronous** and **asynchronous** sound.
- Reconstruct what a sequence probably shows from its soundtrack alone, and explain what that proves about sound.
- Explain how **non-diegetic** music alone can move a text between genres.
- Design, record, and post a 30-second soundscape for your own opening, justifying each choice (CS4).

## Practice

Sound terms are defined in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary and listed in full on the [[concepts/technical-elements|technical elements]] page.

**1.** A character switches on a car radio and a song plays. Diegetic or non-diegetic? Now the song continues over the next scene, in a kitchen, with no radio. What has happened?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

In the car it is **diegetic**: the song exists in the world and the character can hear it. Carrying it into the kitchen turns it **non-diegetic**, and the carry itself is a **sound bridge**. Texts cross that line deliberately, and naming the crossing is worth more than naming either state.

</details>

**2.** Define a sound bridge in one sentence.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Audio from one scene begins before, or continues after, the cut that changes the picture. It ties two spaces or times together through the ear rather than the eye.

</details>

**3.** Which of these is **not** in the syllabus's sound list?

- a) Sound perspective
- b) Sound motif
- c) Match on action

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

c) **Match on action** is editing, not sound. Misfiling it is a common and costly error; the mark scheme reads the category you put it in.

</details>

**4.** The Scheme of Work says one technical element is routinely neglected by candidates. Which, and why does that matter to you?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Sound.** It matters because neglect is an opportunity: an extract analysis that handles sound with precise terms stands out against a field that mostly does not.

</details>

**5.** Watch two minutes of anything with your eyes closed. List every sound, then sort your list into diegetic and non-diegetic, then mark which one you would have missed with your eyes open.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The one you would have missed is almost always ambient sound or the score. Both are doing continuous work on your mood, which is exactly why they are easy to overlook and worth naming.

</details>

**6.** Take the soundscape you built. Replace only the non-diegetic element and change nothing else. Post both versions and write one sentence on what genre each now promises.
