## Overview

This lesson teaches representation theory and then turns it on your own work. You submit [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a2-blog-mid-point|A2]], your C1 blog mid-point, on Mon 2026-10-26. Over the week you learn how representation is built, how audiences read it, and how it uses stereotypes and absence. The week closes with two standalone sessions: your rough cut is screened as an audience would see it, then you audit it for the representations you did not notice. The audit produces a fix list of production pickups, and it happens now because there is still production time left. This feeds [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a3-c1-portfolio|A3]].

## The ideas

### Construction, not reflection

Representation does not reflect the world. It **constructs** a version of it. It builds that version through **selection**, what you choose to include, and **combination**, how you put those choices together. The coursebook (§3.3) says representation is "never objective".

History shows this predates the word. Lincoln and McClellan posed in a tent at Antietam in 1862: the tent flaps, the table, who sits, every choice does representation work. Take one public figure and three found images, an official portrait, a news photo, and a meme or fan edit. Each selects and combines differently, and each asks you to conclude something different. The person never changed. The construction did.

### Hall's reading positions

**Encoding/decoding** (Stuart Hall): the maker encodes a preferred meaning, and audiences decode it from one of three positions. In the **dominant** position you accept the preferred meaning. In the **negotiated** position you accept part and resist part. In the **oppositional** position you reject it. Meaning is finished in the audience, not in the text, so audiences will not agree. That disagreement is the theory working.

Turn this inward: ask what reading position your C1 product assumes its audience will take, and whether it could survive an oppositional reading.

### Stereotyping as power

A **stereotype** is not a lie. It is a shortcut with a beneficiary: someone benefits from it and someone pays. A **counter-stereotype**, such as Carl in *UP*, registers as surprising, and that surprise shows you the default. Work two contrasting cases from exam-eligible international media, one from British TV and one from Hollywood film: name the shortcut, its history, and its beneficiary, then run Hall's positions across both to see who reads each one dominantly and who oppositionally.

**Nation branding** is stereotyping at scale. Work *Downton Abbey* Britain as the exam case: the selections the "Britain brand" makes for global audiences, and what each one leaves out. As a spoken aside, contrast what a "China brand" would select.

### Absence

**Absence** is selective construction's quietest tool. What is never in the frame cannot be argued with, and who never appears cannot complain. Inventory a text for its absences: who is missing, what work each absence does, and what would change if it were repaired. Then run the same inventory on your own C1 planning: who is absent from your product, and is that a choice or a default?

## Review

Check you can:

- Explain construction versus reflection using an example from your own product.
- Describe how selection and combination build a representation.
- Place a real reading of a text into one of Hall's three positions and justify it with evidence.
- Name who a chosen stereotype works for and what work it does.
- Identify an absence in a text and say what work that absence does.
- Audit your own rough cut, naming at least one unintended representation and one planned fix.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. Have your Hall theory card beside you; representation itself is defined in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Representation constructs rather than reflects. Name the two operations it constructs with, and prove the point with a photograph from 1862.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Selection** and **combination**. Lincoln and McClellan posed in a tent at Antietam in 1862: the tent flaps, the table, who sits, all chosen and arranged. The photo-op is older than the word. A representation is never objective, because someone selected and combined it.

</details>

**2.** Name Hall's three reading positions, and state where he says meaning is finished.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Dominant** (you take the preferred meaning), **negotiated** (you take some and adjust the rest), **oppositional** (you read against it). Meaning is finished in the audience, not in the text. That is why a room honestly disagrees about the same text.

</details>

**3.** A stereotype is not a lie. What is it, and what two questions does that definition hand you?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A shortcut with a beneficiary. Ask: who benefits from this shortcut, and who pays for it? That turns spotting a stereotype from a complaint into an analysis. It also explains why a counter-stereotype registers as surprising at all.

</details>

**4.** Why is absence the quietest tool of selective construction?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

What is never in the frame cannot be argued with, and who never appears cannot complain. An absence does its work without leaving evidence on screen, so you have to inventory for it deliberately: who is missing, and what work does the missing do?

</details>

**5.** Take one public figure and three images of them: an official portrait, a news photo, and a meme or fan edit. For each, note what it selects, what it combines, and what it asks you to conclude.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

No fixed answer. The test of a good response: the person never changes across the three images, and your three conclusions still differ. That gap is construction at work.

</details>

**6.** Finish the line "My C1 product represents... as..." Then audit it with the week's toolkit: what did we select and combine, what reading did we assume, whose shortcut did we borrow, who is missing? Convert one finding into a production pickup while there is still time to shoot it.
