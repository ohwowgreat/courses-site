## Overview

This lesson teaches representation theory, then turns it on your own product. You submit [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a2-blog-mid-point|A2]], your C1 blog mid-point, by URL on Monday 2026-10-26. Across the week you learn how representation is built. On Monday 11-02 you screen your rough cut under audience conditions while peers fill in response cards ([[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|LB3]]). The audit of what the audience saw opens Lesson 12, while there is still production time left to fix problems. This work feeds [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a3-c1-portfolio|A3]].

## 1 · Learn

### Construction, not reflection

**Representation** does not reflect the world. It **constructs** a version of it, through **selection** (what you choose to include) and **combination** (how you arrange those choices). The coursebook (§3.3) says it is "never objective". A historical example: at Antietam in 1862, Lincoln and McClellan posed in a tent. Every choice, the tent flaps, the table, who sits, does representation work. The photo-op is older than the word. Take one public figure and three images of them, an official portrait, a news photo, and a meme or fan edit: the person never changed, but each version selects, combines, and asks you to conclude something different.

### Hall's positions

**Hall's encoding/decoding** model: the maker encodes a preferred meaning, and audiences decode it from one of three positions. A **dominant** reading accepts the preferred meaning. A **negotiated** reading part accepts and part resists it. An **oppositional** reading rejects it. The meaning is finished in the audience, not in the text. So people reading the same text will not agree, and that disagreement is the theory working.

### Stereotyping as power

A **stereotype** is not a lie. It is a shortcut with a beneficiary: some group benefits, and some group pays. Ask who benefits and who pays. A **counter-stereotype** breaks the shortcut, which is why it registers as surprising at all: Carl in *UP* is an example. Work stereotype cases from exam-eligible international media, one from British TV and one from Hollywood film: identify the shortcut, its history, and its beneficiary, then run Hall's positions across each, asking who reads it dominantly and who oppositionally. A Chinese-media parallel can be mentioned as an aside, but the worked cases stay exam-facing.

### Nation branding

**Nation branding** is representation at the scale of a country. *Downton Abbey* Britain works as the exam-facing case: the "Britain brand" makes selections for global audiences, and each selection leaves something out. As a short aside, consider what a "China brand" would select, for contrast.

### Absence

**Absence** is selective construction's quietest tool. What is never in the frame cannot be argued with. Who never appears cannot complain. Inventory a text for its absences: who is missing, what work each absence does, and what would change if it were repaired. Then run the same inventory on your own C1 planning: who is absent from your product, and is that a choice or a default?

## 3 · Check

Check you can:

- Explain construction versus reflection using an example from your own product.
- Place a real reading of a text into one of Hall's dominant, negotiated, or oppositional positions and justify it.
- Name who a stereotype works for and what work it does.
- Identify the absences in a text and say what each one does.
- Screen your rough cut under audience conditions and give peers usable response cards.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. Have your Hall theory card beside you; representation itself is defined in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Representation constructs rather than reflects. Name the two operations it constructs with, and prove the point with a photograph from 1862.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Selection** and **combination**. Lincoln and McClellan posed in a tent at Antietam in 1862: the tent flaps, the table, who sits, all chosen and arranged. The photo-op is older than the word. A representation is never objective, because someone selected and combined it.

</details>

**2.** Name Hall's three reading positions, and state where he says meaning is finished.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Dominant** (you take the preferred meaning), **negotiated** (you take some and adjust the rest), **oppositional** (you read against it). Meaning is finished in the audience, not in the text. That is why a room honestly disagrees about the same text.

</details>

**3.** A stereotype is not a lie. What is it, and what two questions does that definition hand you?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A shortcut with a beneficiary. Ask: who benefits from this shortcut, and who pays for it? That turns spotting a stereotype from a complaint into an analysis. It also explains why a counter-stereotype registers as surprising at all.

</details>

**4.** Why is absence the quietest tool of selective construction?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

What is never in the frame cannot be argued with, and who never appears cannot complain. An absence does its work without leaving evidence on screen, so you have to inventory for it deliberately: who is missing, and what work does the missing do?

</details>

**5.** Take one public figure and three images of them: an official portrait, a news photo, and a meme or fan edit. For each, note what it selects, what it combines, and what it asks you to conclude.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

No fixed answer. The test of a good response: the person never changes across the three images, and your three conclusions still differ. That gap is construction at work.

</details>

**6.** Finish the line "My C1 product represents... as..." Then audit it with the week's toolkit: what did we select and combine, what reading did we assume, whose shortcut did we borrow, who is missing? Convert one finding into a production pickup while there is still time to shoot it.
