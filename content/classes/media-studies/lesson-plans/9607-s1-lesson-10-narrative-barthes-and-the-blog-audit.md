## The ideas

### Todorov: the narrative arc

**Todorov's model** describes a narrative as a movement from equilibrium, through disruption, to a new equilibrium. The full arc is: equilibrium, disruption, recognition, repair, new equilibrium. Track a story you know onto this arc.

The model has a failure condition, and naming it earns marks. Todorov's model fails on non-linear narratives. A top-band answer discusses where a model strains. Saying "the model fits perfectly" is neither true nor interesting.

### Propp: character functions

**Propp's model** classifies characters by what they do, not who they are. The functions are: hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, false hero. Propp built this from folk tales in 1928, so apply it with judgment.

**Binary oppositions** are the conflicts of value a narrative runs on, not just conflicts between people. See the worked *Lupin* table for how this maps. Narratives run on opposed values as much as on opposed characters.

### The strain log

Keep a **strain log**, a running list titled "Where the theory strains." It records the points where each model fails to fit. This is exam ammunition, not a complaint box.

### Barthes: enigma and action codes

**Barthes** supplies two sharp tools. The **enigma code** is the question a text plants and refuses to answer yet. The **action code** is the promise that something will happen, and soon. Watch a TV cold open twice: first list every question the text plants, then list every promise it makes. Openings are engineered to owe you answers. These join Barthes's earlier tools, anchorage and connotation: one theorist, many tools.

### The exam sentence and answer shape

Rehearse one sentence until it is automatic: "Todorov's model illuminates X, although the extract complicates it by Y." The second half is where the marks are.

A Section A response has a clear shape: an introduction that names the argument, one paragraph per element in the reading order, and a conclusion that answers the prompt's verb. Build the skeleton first, then the prose. This is built properly at [[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-15-institutions-and-section-a-craft|Lesson 15]].

## Review

Check you can:

- Track a narrative using **Todorov's model** as equilibrium, disruption, and new equilibrium on a text you were not taught with.
- Map characters by function using **Propp's model**, and name where Propp strains.
- Explain **binary oppositions** as conflicts of value in a narrative.
- Identify the **enigma code** and the **action code** in a text, and say what each withholds or promises.
- Audit a blog against the mid-point checklist and write your findings as actions.
- Leave with a written action list for your own blog.

## Practice

Terms live in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary and on the [[concepts/narrative|narrative]] concept page.

**1.** Put Todorov's five stages in order: repair, disruption, new equilibrium, equilibrium, recognition.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Equilibrium, disruption, recognition, repair, new equilibrium. The story starts settled, something breaks it, someone sees the break, someone acts on it, and it settles again changed.

</details>

**2.** Todorov's model has a known weakness. Name it, and say what to do about it in an exam.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

It fails on non-linear narratives. A story told out of order, or one that never restores balance, will not map cleanly. Saying so, with the place it strains named, earns marks. Pretending the model always fits does not.

</details>

**3.** Propp classifies characters by one rule. State the rule, then name three functions.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

By what they do, not who they are. Functions include the hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher and false hero. Attach the caveat when you use him: folk tales, 1928, apply with judgment.

</details>

**4.** Distinguish the enigma code from the action code.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The **enigma code** plants a question and refuses to answer it yet. The **action code** promises that something will happen, and soon. One makes you lean in with a question, the other with anticipation.

</details>

**5.** A trailer never shows its villain. What is happening in Propp's terms, and why would a marketer do it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A function is withheld. The cast maps onto Propp except for the villain, and the gap is deliberate: the trailer plants a question it refuses to answer so you buy a ticket to close it.

</details>

**6.** Narratives run on binary oppositions: conflicts of value, not just people. Give one from a text you know, as "x versus y".

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Any genuine pair works: order versus chaos, law versus crime, home versus away, youth versus age. The test is that scenes, costumes and settings line up on the two sides.

</details>

**7.** Audit your own blog before Monday. For each post ask the checklist's question: is it dated on the day it happened, is your contribution labelled, and would a stranger know what it is evidence of?
