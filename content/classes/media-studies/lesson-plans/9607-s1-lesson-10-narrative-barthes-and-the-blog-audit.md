## Overview

This lesson teaches the second macro element, narrative, and runs the audit that gets you ready for [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a2-blog-mid-point|A2]]. You study three models: Todorov and Propp for how stories are built, and Barthes for what a text withholds and promises. Your deliverable is **[[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|CS9]]**, a peer blog audit against the mid-point checklist, run in the Wednesday double on 2026-10-21, three teaching days before A2 is due on Monday 10-26. The audit uses the exact checklist A2 is marked with, so you leave with a written action list and time to act on it. Narrative feeds A2, [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a4-section-a-authentic-format|A4]], and the End of Term Exam.

## 1 · Learn

### Barthes: enigma and action codes

The **enigma code** is a question a text plants and refuses to answer yet. The **action code** is a promise that something will happen, and soon. In a TV cold open, watch it twice: on the first pass, list every question the text plants; on the second, every promise it makes. Openings are engineered to owe you answers. This is Barthes' third tool for this course, after anchorage and connotation: one theorist, many tools.

### Todorov and Propp

**Todorov's** narrative arc runs equilibrium → disruption → recognition → repair → new equilibrium. It fails on non-linear narratives, and saying so earns marks, so the limit is part of the model, not an afterthought.

**Propp** classifies characters by what they do, not who they are: hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, false hero. This comes from folk tales in 1928, so apply it with judgment.

A key principle: **a top-band answer discusses where a model strains.** "The model fits perfectly" is neither true nor interesting. Keep a **strain log**, a running list titled "Where the theory strains." It is exam ammunition, not a complaint box. On a contemporary trailer, Propp strains in useful ways: a false hero who is also the donor, or a trailer that hides its villain on purpose, because a withheld function is a marketing decision.

### The audit and A2

The mid-point checklist mirrors the A2 register entry exactly, so **the audit is A2's marking preview.** The checklist goes post by post: brief and group; research; audience profile; pitch; planning suite; the four technical practice posts; design board; production log; early evidence. Every post must be dated, contributions labelled, and no gaps left. Swap blogs and audit post by post using the protocol **describe → question → suggest**, and write findings as actions: "post 3 has no audience data, add the survey screenshot" beats "more detail".

### The exam sentence and Section A shape

Rehearse this sentence until it is automatic: *"Todorov's model illuminates X, although the extract complicates it by Y."* The second half is where the marks are. A Section A response has an introduction that names the argument, one paragraph per element in the reading order, and a conclusion that answers the prompt's verb. Build the skeleton first, then the prose. You build this shape properly at [[classes/media-studies/lesson-plans/9607-s1-lesson-15-institutions-and-section-a-craft|Lesson 15]].

## Practice

Terms live in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary and on the [[concepts/narrative|narrative]] concept page.

**1.** Put Todorov's five stages in order: repair, disruption, new equilibrium, equilibrium, recognition.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Equilibrium, disruption, recognition, repair, new equilibrium. The story starts settled, something breaks it, someone sees the break, someone acts on it, and it settles again changed.

</details>

**2.** Todorov's model has a known weakness. Name it, and say what to do about it in an exam.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

It fails on non-linear narratives. A story told out of order, or one that never restores balance, will not map cleanly. Saying so, with the place it strains named, earns marks. Pretending the model always fits does not.

</details>

**3.** Propp classifies characters by one rule. State the rule, then name three functions.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

By what they do, not who they are. Functions include the hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher and false hero. Attach the caveat when you use him: folk tales, 1928, apply with judgment.

</details>

**4.** Distinguish the enigma code from the action code.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The **enigma code** plants a question and refuses to answer it yet. The **action code** promises that something will happen, and soon. One makes you lean in with a question, the other with anticipation.

</details>

**5.** A trailer never shows its villain. What is happening in Propp's terms, and why would a marketer do it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A function is withheld. The cast maps onto Propp except for the villain, and the gap is deliberate: the trailer plants a question it refuses to answer so you buy a ticket to close it.

</details>

**6.** Narratives run on binary oppositions: conflicts of value, not just people. Give one from a text you know, as "x versus y".

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Any genuine pair works: order versus chaos, law versus crime, home versus away, youth versus age. The test is that scenes, costumes and settings line up on the two sides.

</details>

**7.** Audit your own blog before Monday. For each post ask the checklist's question: is it dated on the day it happened, is your contribution labelled, and would a stranger know what it is evidence of?
