## Overview

This is your first attainment task. On Monday and Tuesday you put two narrative theories under load on real texts, hunting for where each one strains. On Wednesday you sit [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]]: a 60-minute timed media language analysis of an unseen TV-drama extract, written under full exam conditions. Your script is due when you write it in class on Wed 2026-10-14. It draws on everything since Week 1 and feeds AS Component 2 Section A.

## The ideas

### Propp under load

**Propp** describes stories through a set of character functions, such as hero, villain, donor, and false hero. Map the cast of one contemporary trailer onto these functions. Then ask the harder question in writing: where did Propp fail to fit, and why is that interesting? A false hero can also be the donor. A trailer can hide its villain on purpose, and a **withheld function** is a marketing decision, not a gap in the theory.

### Todorov under load

**Todorov** describes narrative as movement through five stages: from an equilibrium, through a disruption, toward a new equilibrium. Map a full episode against these stages. Ask where the disruption really is, whether the new equilibrium arrives or is withheld for the season arc, and what a cold open does to the model. Serial television bends Todorov, so name the bend.

### The strain log

**Strain** is any place where a theory does not fit the text cleanly. Keep a running list titled "Where the theory strains." A top-band answer discusses strain, because "the model fits perfectly" is almost never true or interesting. Turn a strain entry into an exam sentence like this: "Todorov's model illuminates X, although the extract complicates it by Y."

### Writing the sit

Bring a **personal code checklist** to the sit and use it: systematic coverage in your script is a success criterion. Follow the screening protocol of four viewings: one first watch, then three viewings with note passes. Aim for observations that are technical, symbolic, and narrative, each tied to an effect rather than listed. Reach across your code families and notice which one you forget first.

## Review

Check you can:

- Apply Propp's character functions to a trailer and name at least one point of strain.
- Apply Todorov's five stages to an episode and name where serial television bends the model.
- Explain why a withheld function can be a marketing decision.

## Practice

Use these before the sit, and again after it as revision. Terms live in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Why do we hunt for the places where Propp and Todorov strain, instead of showing they fit?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Because strain is what a top-band answer discusses. "The model fits perfectly" is almost never true, and never interesting. One sentence naming where the theory bends, and why, is worth more than a clean mapping.

</details>

**2.** A trailer never shows its villain. What is happening, in Propp's terms, and why would a marketer do it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A function is withheld. The cast maps onto Propp except for the villain, and the gap is deliberate: the trailer plants a question it refuses to answer so you buy a ticket to close it. A withheld function is a marketing decision.

</details>

**3.** Complete the exam sentence on a text you know: "Todorov's model illuminates ___, although the extract complicates it by ___."

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Any honest pair works. Example shape: "Todorov's model illuminates the pilot's move from settled routine to disruption, although the episode complicates it by withholding the new equilibrium for the season arc." The second half is where the marks are.

</details>

**4.** State the screening protocol for the sit: how many viewings, and what is each for?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Four viewings. The first is a straight watch. The next three are note passes, one focus at a time, so coverage is built in rather than left to memory under pressure.

</details>

**5.** What does "systematic coverage" mean in your script, and what tool proves you have it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Every code family visited: technical, symbolic, written, audio, plus narrative structure. Your personal code checklist is the tool. A script that reads camera brilliantly but never mentions sound has a coverage gap, and the marker sees it.

</details>

**6.** Re-run the sit at home as revision. Choose any unseen two-to-three-minute drama extract. Sixty minutes, exam conditions, the exam prompt: how does the extract construct meaning through camera, sound, mise-en-scène, and editing? Then mark yourself for coverage only: which code family is missing, and which theory-strain sentence did you manage?
