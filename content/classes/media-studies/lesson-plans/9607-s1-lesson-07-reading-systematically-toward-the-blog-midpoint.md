## Overview

This lesson turns analytical instinct into method. You learn two more of Barthes's codes, a fixed reading order for any text, and the PEEM paragraph. On Wednesday 10-21 you produce your deliverable: the [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|CS5]] peer blog audit, where a partner checks your blog against the same mid-point checklist that [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a2-blog-mid-point|A2]] will be marked with. You get this three teaching days before A2 is due, so you still have time to fix what the audit finds. Your blog, posts 1–7, must be to standard by Mon 10-26.

## The ideas

### Barthes's codes

The **enigma code** is the question a text plants and refuses to answer yet. It makes you lean in. A figure on a rock with his back turned makes you ask *who is he? what does he see?* That pull is the enigma code working.

The **action code** is the promise that something will happen, and soon. It tells you a payoff is coming.

Both appear in a TV cold open. Watch one twice: first list every question the text plants, then list every promise it makes. Openings are built to owe you answers. This is Barthes's third tool in the course, after anchorage and connotation: one theorist, many tools.

### The systematic reading order

The **systematic reading order** is one fixed order you run on every extract, every time: framing, then camera, then light, then mise-en-scène, then sound, then edit, then narrative codes. It fixes the commonest [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]] gap, which is coverage: whole code families never visited. The fix is procedural, not talent. Run the order silently, take notes in order, do not wander.

This is where the **Ladder**, your reading routine, arrives in full. The reading order is rungs 1 and 2; PEEM climbs rungs 3 to 5. Copy the order onto your personal code checklist.

### PEEM

**PEEM** is the paragraph that proves instead of asserts: **P**oint, **E**vidence, **E**ffect, **M**eaning. Keep the claim, pin the evidence to a signifier (rung 2), state the effect on the viewer (rung 3), then tie the meaning to the concept (rung 4). An assertive paragraph only claims; a PEEM paragraph shows its work.

The **pairing sentence** drills this: write one sentence that fuses technique and content, five times, fast. Speed over polish.

### The mid-point audit

The **mid-point checklist** goes through your blog post by post: brief and group, research, audience profile, pitch, planning suite, production log, and early evidence. Every post must be dated, contributions labeled, no gaps. This checklist mirrors the A2 register entry exactly, so the audit is your A2 marking preview.

The audit protocol is **describe, question, suggest**. Write findings as actions, not comments. "Post 3 has no audience data, add the survey screenshot" is better than "more detail."

### The shape of an answer

**Section A response architecture** is the plan for a full answer: an introduction that names the argument, one paragraph per code family in the reading order, and a conclusion that answers the prompt's verb. Build the skeleton first, then the prose. The plan is where marks are won.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. Terms live in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Barthes gives you two narrative codes this week. Name both, and say what each one does to you.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The **enigma code** plants a question and refuses to answer it yet: who is he, what does he see. The **action code** promises that something will happen, and soon. One makes you lean in with a question, the other with anticipation. Openings are engineered to owe you answers.

</details>

**2.** Write the systematic reading order from memory.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Framing, camera, light, mise-en-scène, sound, edit, narrative codes. Same order, every extract, every time. The order is the cure for the commonest A1 gap: whole code families left unvisited.

</details>

**3.** Expand PEEM, and say what the M adds that the second E has not already given.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Point, Evidence, Effect, Meaning. Effect says what the technique does to the viewer. Meaning ties that effect back to a Key Concept: what the text is saying about people, power, or the world. Effect is rung three of the Ladder. Meaning is rung four.

</details>

**4.** Rebuild this assertive sentence as a PEEM paragraph: "The opening is tense."

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

One strong shape: the opening builds tension (Point). The camera holds a slow track toward a closed door while the score drops to a single held note (Evidence, pinned to signifiers). The viewer waits for the door because the text has promised something behind it (Effect). Tension here is how the drama positions its audience as investigator (Meaning).

</details>

**5.** A blog can have every post dated, every contribution labeled, and still be in trouble at the mid-point. How?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The ratio. Cambridge's own marked exemplar capped partly for carrying too much preliminary material and too little on the set brief. Ask of your own blog: how much of this is about the actual brief? Dating rules are necessary. They are not sufficient.

</details>

**6.** Run the reading order silently on one new still: notes in order, no wandering, one observation per stop. Then write one PEEM paragraph from the best observation. This is the exact discipline A2's marker wants to see growing in your posts.
