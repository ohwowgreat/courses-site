## Overview

This lesson tests your technical vocabulary, then puts it to work under exam conditions. On Mon 10-12 you sit **[[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|CS8]]**, the technical lexicon quiz, and you learn the paragraph frame you will use to write. On Wed 10-14 you sit **[[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]]**, a 60-minute analysis of an unseen TV-drama extract written under full exam conditions. On Thu 10-15 you run the same format again with the stakes off, while the sit is still fresh. Everything you have practiced since Week 1 is in play, and it feeds AS Component 2 Section A.

## 1 · Learn

### The technical lexicon (CS8)

**CS8** is a quiz of thirty items. You define terms and label examples drawn across all four technical elements, camera, sound, mise-en-scène and editing, plus the semiotic base. Sound and editing carry weight here, because those are the two families that scripts most often drop.

### The viewing protocol

The **viewing protocol** is a fixed order for watching an extract so your coverage is systematic. For A1 you use the exam's own screening rubric: four viewings, first a straight watch, then three note passes, with a cut count added on a later pass. Working in this order is a success criterion in the script.

### PEEM

**PEEM** is the paragraph frame. It is four distinct moves, not one move said twice:

- **P**oint: the claim about what the extract is doing.
- **E**vidence: pinned to a named signifier, in the correct technical term.
- **E**ffect: what it does to the viewer.
- **M**eaning: what it therefore says about the person, place or event represented.

The common error is to write the effect and the meaning as the same sentence twice. Keep them as separate moves.

### The Ladder

**The Ladder** connects the two. The reading order runs rungs 1 to 2, and PEEM climbs rungs 3 to 5.

### Beat the Examiner

**Beat the Examiner** is a banding routine. You read an anonymized A1-style paragraph and call its band against the five official Paper 2 criteria: concepts, contexts, terminology, analysis and examples. When your own A1 scripts return, you band them with the same grid.

## 2 · Do

- **Mon 10-12.** You sit CS8, thirty items in twenty minutes, then self-mark immediately against the definitions on screen and correct any missed terms into your lexicon. You then learn PEEM through one worked example and one deliberately bad example to diagnose. Blog posts 1 to 7 are due Mon 10-26, brought to the mid-point standard.
- **Wed 10-14 (double).** You sit A1: an unseen TV-drama extract, 60 minutes, full exam conditions, answering how the extract constructs meaning through camera, sound, mise-en-scène and editing. A structured debrief follows, with no marks discussed.
- **Thu 10-15.** You recall the protocol order cold, then read a fresh unseen extract played twice, take notes in protocol order with a cut count, and write one PEEM paragraph to time. You run Beat the Examiner on one paragraph, then add one term to your lexicon: the word you reached for on Wednesday and did not have.

## 3 · Check

Check you can:

- Define thirty terms from all four technical elements without notes.
- Run the **viewing protocol** in its fixed order and evidence systematic coverage in a script.
- Write a **PEEM** paragraph in which the effect and the meaning are distinct moves.
- Sustain 60 minutes of continuous analytical writing under exam conditions.
- Tie camera, sound, mise-en-scène and editing observations to effects rather than listing them.
- Band an A1-style paragraph against the five official Paper 2 criteria.

## Practice

Use these before the sit, and again after it as revision. Terms live in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** State the viewing protocol's order, and say what it is a cure for.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Framing, camera, light, mise-en-scène, sound, edit. Same order, every extract, every time. It cures the commonest gap in a script: a whole code family never visited, which the marker sees immediately.

</details>

**2.** Name the four moves of PEEM and say which two students most often collapse into one.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Point, Evidence, Effect, Meaning. **Effect and Meaning** are the pair that collapses. Effect is what it does to the viewer; Meaning is what it therefore says about the person, place or event. If your two sentences could swap places, you have written one move twice.

</details>

**3.** State the screening protocol for the sit: how many viewings, and what is each for?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Four viewings. The first is a straight watch. The next three are note passes, one focus at a time, so coverage is built in rather than left to memory under pressure.

</details>

**4.** What does "systematic coverage" mean in your script, and what tool proves you have it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Every code family visited: technical, symbolic, written, audio. Your personal code checklist is the tool. A script that reads camera brilliantly but never mentions sound has a coverage gap, and the marker sees it.

</details>

**5.** Rewrite as a full PEEM paragraph: "There is a low angle on the detective and it makes him look powerful."

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Point: the extract establishes the detective's authority before he speaks. Evidence: a low-angle mid-shot holds him against the ceiling line while the suspect is framed in a high-angle close-up. Effect: the viewer is placed beneath him and above the suspect in turn. Meaning: the scene constructs institutional power as a physical fact of the room rather than something argued for.

</details>

**6.** Re-run the sit at home as revision. Any unseen two to three minute drama extract, sixty minutes, exam conditions, the exam prompt. Then mark yourself for coverage only: which of the four elements is missing?
