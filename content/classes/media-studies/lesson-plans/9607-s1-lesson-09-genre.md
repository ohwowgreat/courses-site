## Overview

This lesson introduces genre, the first of the macro elements. You learn to treat genre not as a list of tropes but as an agreement between the industry and the audience. You then evidence a genre from codes alone, and you map the genre contract onto your own C1 product, posting it to the blog as a research entry. That post feeds [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a2-blog-mid-point|A2]], [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a4-section-a-authentic-format|A4]] and the C2 case-study document. Every C3 brief you meet later is a genre contract before it is a production.

## The ideas

### Genre as a contract

Genre is a negotiated agreement, not a fixed set of tropes. A trope list earns nothing. The exam-usable version reframes genre as a deal between two sides.

**Neale**: genre works through repetition and difference. Each text repeats familiar elements and adds something new. Neale calls this "difference in repetition."

**Altman**: genre is an agreement between industry and audience. The industry gets a pre-sold, budgetable product. The audience gets a promise of pleasure, with room for surprise. Each side receives something from the deal.

You can see genre working before anyone teaches you the rules. Two paintings of skulls and candles, painted centuries apart by different artists, still read as belonging together. That instant recognition is genre.

For stretch, the coursebook adds three more theorists:

- **Lacey**: a genre is a repertoire of elements.
- **Buckingham**: genres negotiate and change over time.
- **Fiske**: genre gives both structure and convenience.

### Evidencing genre from codes

Attribute a genre and defend it from codes alone. Use the micro elements you have spent a month shooting: lighting, shot length, sound design. "The lighting is low key and the cut rate doubles at the reveal" is a stronger answer than "it feels scary." A genre claim defended through codes earns marks; a claim defended through plot content does not.

### Hybridity

**Hybridity**: genres are not boxes. Some texts sit across two genres. Name such a text and say what each parent genre contributes.

## Day by day

- **Fri 10-16.** Genre is a contract. Learn Neale (repetition and difference) and Altman (genre as the deal). Map one exam-plausible genre you know well (horror, crime drama, sitcom, documentary, reality TV) onto the contract: what repeats, what varies, who profits from the repetition, what the audience is promised. A C-drama or xianxia mapping is fine as a spoken aside; keep your written mapping exam-facing.
- **Mon 10-19.** Prove genre from codes, then turn it on your own product. Watch four short unlabeled clips and match each to one of four candidate genres, defending your choice from codes alone with two pieces of evidence. Then post your genre-contract research entry to the blog: what genre your opening offers, what it promises the audience, which three codes carry the promise, and what you will vary so the text is not a copy. No homework, as A2 falls Mon 10-26.

## Review

Check you can:

- Explain genre as a negotiated agreement, using repetition and difference, without reciting a trope list.
- State what each side gets in Altman's industry-audience contract.
- Attribute a genre to an unlabeled clip and evidence it from codes alone.
- Explain hybridity and name a text that sits across two genres.
- State in writing the contract your own product offers its audience.

## Practice

Terms are defined in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Neale says genre works through two things at once. Name both, and say why a text needs each one.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Repetition and difference.** Repetition lets you recognise the genre and get what you were promised. Difference keeps the text from being a copy you have no reason to watch.

</details>

**2.** Altman calls genre a deal between industry and audience. What does each side get?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The industry gets a pre-sold, budgetable product: it knows roughly what the text costs and who will buy it. The audience gets a promise of pleasure with room for surprise. This contract version of genre earns marks. A trope list does not.

</details>

**3.** Defend a genre attribution for a clip with the sound off, using only technical codes.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A strong answer names two or three: the lighting style (high or low key), the shot length and cut rate, the colour palette, the framing of faces. "It looks scary" is not evidence. "Low key lighting, faces held in shallow focus, eleven cuts in twenty seconds" is.

</details>

**4.** What is a genre hybrid, and why do studios make them?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A text that draws on the repertoire of two genres at once. Studios make them to reach two audiences with one budget while still promising each of them something recognisable.

</details>

**5.** State your own product's contract in two sentences: what it promises, and what it varies.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

No single right answer, but both halves must be there. A promise with nothing varied is pastiche; variation with nothing promised is a text nobody can find.

</details>

**6.** Watch sixty seconds of something unfamiliar with the sound off. Attribute a genre from codes alone and note two pieces of evidence. Then watch it again with sound and check whether the audio agreed with you.
