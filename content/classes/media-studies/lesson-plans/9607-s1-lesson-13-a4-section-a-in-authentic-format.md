## Overview

This lesson traces one institution from end to end: how it integrates, where it uses synergy, and how it converges onto new platforms. On Wed 12-16 you sit [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a4-section-a-authentic-format|A4]], the first Section A response in its real format: 30 minutes of viewing and note-making, then 45 minutes of writing. This is the anchor for your Forecast Grade, and it feeds the End of Term Exam. Friday adds regulation as the fourth media context while the sit is still fresh.

## The ideas

### The three institution words

**Integration** means owning more of the process. Vertical integration is owning the stages of production and distribution. Horizontal integration is owning the neighbors, other companies at the same stage. **Synergy** is when the parts of a company sell each other. **Convergence** is when different media and platforms flow together.

The historical anchor is Charles Lindbergh's flight in 1927: one man, and every channel of the era sold the same story to itself through newsreels, press syndicates, radio, and records. The planned media event predates the internet by seventy years.

### Following an institution end to end

You can follow one institution from decision to screen as a single chain: who greenlights it, who produces it, who distributes it, how it is marketed, where audiences meet it, and who takes the revenue. Each link tells you something about how the institution works.

### Structured notes

The viewing window in A4 is for structured notes, not a transcript. Use a grid of four families of notes, not full sentences. The structured-notes drill practices this: watch a short clip, fill the grid, stop. This is a different skill from [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]]'s single hour of writing.

### Regulation

Regulation is the fourth media context: who regulates the institution, with what powers, and what that visibly changes about its output, such as scheduling, cuts, ratings, and what never gets made. The regulator depends on the institution's home market: Ofcom, the FCC, or platform self-regulation. This extends the chain by one link: decision ← incentive ← payer ← **regulator**. As an aside, the NRTA is China's broadcast regulator; where the case-study institution is exam-eligible, its regulator is too.

## Review

Check you can:

- Define **integration**, **synergy**, and **convergence**, and evidence each from one institution.
- Tell vertical integration from horizontal integration.
- Use the 30-minute viewing window to make structured notes in a grid, not a transcript.
- Sustain the 45-minute write from your notes rather than from memory.
- Trace one institution end to end, from greenlight to revenue.
- Name who regulates your case-study institution and one concrete thing that regulation changes about its output.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. Synergy and convergence are defined in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary.

**1.** Define the exam's three institution words so they cannot be confused with each other.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Integration** is ownership: vertical when a company owns the stages of production, horizontal when it owns its neighbors. **Synergy** is parts selling each other: the film, the soundtrack, the game, the ride. **Convergence** is media and platforms flowing together, so one device or service carries what used to be many.

</details>

**2.** What does Lindbergh in 1927 prove about media events?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

One man, and every channel of the era selling the same story to itself: newsreels, press syndicates, radio, records. The media event, and synergy with it, predates the internet by seventy years. New platforms change the plumbing, not the pattern.

</details>

**3.** The thirty-minute viewing window: what is it for, and what is the classic mistake?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Structured notes in the four-family grid, so the forty-five minute write runs from notes rather than memory. The classic mistake is a transcript: sentences describing the extract in order, which cost the window and feed nothing. The grid, not prose.

</details>

**4.** Who regulates your case-study institution, and what is one concrete thing regulation visibly changes in its output?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Name the regulator for its home market, such as Ofcom for a UK broadcaster or the FCC in the US, or platform self-regulation. Then the visible consequence: scheduling and the watershed, cuts and ratings, what never gets made. Regulation is the fourth context, and it earns marks only when tied to visible output.

</details>

**5.** Extend the funding chain by one link and read it back.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Decision, incentive, payer, regulator. The cut that keeps a rating (decision) protects the slot (incentive) that the advertiser pays for (payer) inside rules the regulator sets and enforces (regulator). Four links, one sentence each, and Section B of the real paper is already half-planned.

</details>

**6.** Run the structured-notes drill: ninety seconds of any trailer, notes straight into the four-family grid, pens down at two minutes. Repeat on a second trailer, grid tighter. This is the exact discipline the sit's viewing window rewards.
