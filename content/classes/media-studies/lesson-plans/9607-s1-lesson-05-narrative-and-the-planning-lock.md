## Overview

This lesson brings two things together: narrative theory and your production planning. You learn two ways to describe how stories work, then you use them to test your own opening. On Wednesday 09-30 you lock your C1 planning suite on the blog to a shootable standard: a plan someone else could shoot without asking questions. HW1 is set the same day, because the notice window for A1 falls inside the National Day break and cannot wait. After the break, on Friday 10-09, you get your HW1 back annotated, and you meet the sound and editing vocabulary your shoots have just made real. This work feeds A2 and A1.

## The ideas

### Narrative structure: Todorov

**Todorov** describes a narrative as a movement through stages: equilibrium, then disruption, then recognition, then repair, then a new equilibrium. In short: a settled state is disturbed and then resolved into a new settled state. You should be able to track this on a text you have not studied in class. The model has a limit: it fails on non-linear narratives, and naming that limit earns marks. Apply the same test to your own C1 plan by asking where the disturbance is in your opening. If there is none, that is a fix for Wednesday's lock.

### Character function: Propp

**Propp** classifies characters by what they do, not by who they are. The functions are hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, and false hero. The caveat travels with the theory: it comes from a 1928 study of folk tales, so apply it with judgment, and expect it to strain on modern texts. Test it on your own C1 opening by asking who your donor is, or whether you even have one.

**Binary oppositions** are conflicts of value that drive a narrative, not just conflicts between people. A worked *Lupin* table shows how a story runs on opposed values.

### The shootability checklist

The **shootability checklist** is ten items your plan must pass in peer review, framed as launch clearance: ten green lights or the plan does not fly. The items are numbered shots, confirmed locations, cast attached to dates, equipment per session, a contingency day, assigned roles, costume and props, a storyboard that matches the shot list, a schedule that fits the break, and no outside dependencies.

Peer review uses the protocol **describe, question, suggest**: every item is checked or challenged, and "it's fine" does not count.

### The production diary

The **production diary** (WS 6.4) records the decisions you make after the plan is locked. Locked means locked: changes after Wednesday are documented decisions in the diary, not silent swaps. Its decisions and consequences columns are used again at Lesson 10 for the CCR.

### Sound and editing vocabulary

After the break you learn sound and editing terms against your own footage. A **J-cut** is a cut where the sound of the next shot starts before its picture. **Ambient sound** is the background sound of a location, which you often need to control. Look for a J-cut you made by accident, and name the ambient sound you forgot to control.

## Day by day

- **Mon 09-28.** Learn Todorov's arc: work through it on one short narrative, track it on a second text together, then map a story you know. Turn the arc on your own C1 plan and find where the disturbance is.
- **Tue 09-29.** Recall the five stages from memory, then learn Propp's character functions and binary oppositions, with the *Lupin* table. Map your own C1 opening onto the functions. LB1 is posted for blog punctuality (posts 1–5). Ten minutes are reserved in the room to bring lagging blogs current.
- **Wed 09-30 (double).** The planning lock. Another group reviews your planning suite (storyboard or flat plan, shot list, schedule, roles, casting) against the shootability checklist. Apply the fixes, then post and date your final plan on the blog before you leave. You receive the production diary. HW1 is set: an A1-format analysis of a self-chosen 2–3 minute sequence on a teacher-viewable platform, not a taught text, due Fri 10-09. Your break production window is 3–4 hours of shooting, with diary entries as you go. Equipment is confirmed on the way out: hand in your school camera sign-out or name your own tested kit.
- **Fri 10-09 (post-break).** Learn sound and editing vocabulary against your own break footage. HW1 is debriefed, annotated and not graded, with common gaps collected on the board. LB2 is posted: HW1 returned on time, glossary current. A1 is the following Wednesday.

## Review

Check you can:

- Track a narrative as equilibrium, disruption, and new equilibrium using **Todorov**, on a text you were not taught.
- Name where **Todorov** strains, such as non-linear narratives.
- Map characters by function using **Propp**: hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, false hero.
- Name where **Propp** strains, given its 1928 folk-tale origin.
- Explain **binary oppositions** as conflicts of value in a narrative.
- Pass your C1 planning suite against the **shootability checklist** in peer review.
- Record post-lock changes in the **production diary**.
- Name a **J-cut** or an example of **ambient sound** in your own footage.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. The narrative terms are collected in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary and on the [[concepts/narrative|narrative]] concept page.

**1.** Put Todorov's five stages in order: repair, disruption, new equilibrium, equilibrium, recognition.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Equilibrium, disruption, recognition, repair, new equilibrium. The story starts settled, something breaks it, someone sees the break, someone acts on it, and it settles again changed.

</details>

**2.** Todorov's model has a known weakness. Name it, and say what to do about it in an exam.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

It fails on non-linear narratives. A story told out of order, or one that never restores balance, will not map cleanly. Saying so, with the place it strains named, earns marks. Pretending the model always fits does not.

</details>

**3.** Propp classifies characters by one rule. State the rule, then name three of his functions.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

By what they do, not who they are. Functions include the hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, and false hero. Attach the caveat when you use him: the model comes from folk tales, 1928, so apply it with judgment.

</details>

**4.** In a story you know well, who is the donor?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The donor gives the hero what the hero needs to act: an object, a skill, a piece of knowledge. If you cannot find one, say so. Naming where a function is missing is analysis, not failure.

</details>

**5.** Narratives run on binary oppositions: conflicts of value, not just people. Give one from a text you know, in the form "x versus y".

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

Any genuine pair of values works: order versus chaos, law versus crime, home versus away, youth versus age. The test is that scenes, costumes, and settings line up on the two sides.

</details>

**6.** State the planning lock's standard in one sentence, from memory.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

A plan someone else could shoot without asking questions. Numbered shots, confirmed locations, cast attached to dates, tested equipment, a contingency day. If a stranger would need to ask, the plan is not locked.

</details>

**7.** Rehearse HW1 now. Track Todorov's arc on the sequence you chose. If it will not map, write one sentence saying exactly where and why. Bring that sentence into your analysis.
