## Overview

This lesson takes a sign apart and puts it back together. You learn how images carry meaning: the form of a sign, the idea it stands for, what it shows, and what it suggests. You produce **[[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-course-skills-and-homework|CS2]]**, an annotated everyday media text with at least six accurate code labels, set on day 1 and due Fri 2026-09-11. This work feeds [[classes/media-studies/assessments/9607-s1-a1-timed-media-language-analysis|A1]]. On Wednesday you also commit to the media area you will keep working on until May.

## The ideas

### The sign

A **sign** is made of two parts. The **signifier** is the form: the image, word, or sound you actually see or hear. The **signified** is the idea it makes you think of. These terms come from Saussure.

**Denotation** is what an image literally shows. **Connotation** is what it suggests on top of that. On a worked advertisement reading, you separate the two: first what is there, then what it makes you feel or think.

**Polysemy** means one image can carry several legitimate readings at once.

### Connotation is cultural

Connotation is learned, not natural. You build a connotation chain from one signifier (rose → romance → Valentine's → spending), then ask of each link: who taught you that?

Worked on a color wheel from the coursebook (§2.4), against connotation sets a Cambridge extract uses: red (danger or passion), white (purity), black (mourning or luxury), the rose, the dove, the suit. Each connotation traces back to who taught it.

Aside: cultures diverge here. White can mean mourning, red can mean fortune, and the number four carries meaning in one culture and not another. This shows the wheel is culture-bound. Note that the exam asks only about the first set.

### Address

**Address** is who a text thinks you are. To find it, build an **implied-reader profile**: age, gender, income, knowledge assumed, mood invited, and the pronouns used. The evidence for each must come from the text itself.

Address can be formal or informal, direct or indirect. The key point works two ways: texts do not just find audiences, they construct them.

### Anchorage and relay

**Anchorage** (Barthes) is text pinning down which meaning of a polysemic image you take. A caption can steer one image toward sympathy, suspicion, or comedy while the image itself never changes.

**Relay** is when words and image take turns carrying the story.

## Review

Check you can:

- Use **signifier**, **signified**, **denotation**, and **connotation** correctly in your own sentence.
- Explain **polysemy** and why one image can have several readings.
- Show that connotation is cultural by tracing a connotation to who taught it.
- Identify a text's **address** and name the features that prove it.
- Build an **implied-reader profile** from evidence in a text.
- Define **anchorage** and **relay**, and show how a caption steers an image's meaning.
- Annotate a self-chosen everyday text with at least six accurate code labels for CS2.

## Practice

Try each question before you open its answer. Have your Saussure and Barthes theory cards beside you; the terms are collected in the [[concepts/media-vocabulary|media vocabulary]] glossary and your lexicon.

**1.** A sign has two sides. Name both, and say which is which for a red rose.

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

The **signifier** is the form: the rose itself, its shape and color. The **signified** is the idea it triggers: romance, passion. Saussure's split, and the base of every analysis in this course.

</details>

**2.** Which of these is a connotation of the color white, in the set the exam trades in?

- a) A wedding dress photographed against gray
- b) Purity
- c) The lightest value on a tonal scale

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

b) Purity. The dress is a signifier that uses that connotation. The tonal value is denotation territory. Connotation is the cultural meaning a signifier triggers, and it is taught, not natural.

</details>

**3.** One image, several legitimate readings. The term is...

- a) anchorage
- b) polysemy
- c) relay

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

b) **Polysemy**. **Anchorage** is what a caption does about it: text pinning down which of the readings you take. **Relay** is words and image taking turns to carry the story.

</details>

**4.** Take any advertisement you saw today. Answer in two sentences: how old does this text think you are, and what in the text proves it?

<details class="reveal"><summary>Show answer</summary>

No single right answer. A strong response names an address feature as evidence: the pronouns used, the knowledge assumed, the mood invited. That profile is the text's **mode of address**. Texts do not just find audiences. They construct them.

</details>

**5.** Build one connotation chain of at least three links, starting from a signifier in the exam set: red, white, black, the rose, the dove, or the suit. Then challenge one link: who taught you that?

**6.** Write two analytical sentences on any still you choose: one that denotes, one that connotes, with the connotation traced to who taught it. This is the same move CS2 asks you to label six times on your chosen text.
