## Overview

This lesson looks at what happens to an image when it is copied, spread, and degraded. You start with ukiyo-e prints, move to Walter Benjamin on the aura, then to Hito Steyerl on the poor image. On the last day you run the poor-image lab: you degrade an image on purpose, then write exactly what changed. That write-up is CS5, collected Wed 2026-12-09, and it is your practice for A4.

## The ideas

### The print is already many

**Ukiyo-e** are Japanese woodblock prints. A print is many, not one: the block lets the same image be pulled again and again. A market grew up to make and sell these prints in numbers. Because you can already see that a print exists as many copies, Benjamin's argument arrives as a description of something real rather than as abstract theory.

### Aura

**Aura**, a term from Walter Benjamin, is the sense that a work is unique, here now, and tied to one place and moment. Benjamin argues that copying an image strips this quality away. You should be able to define aura closely enough to argue with him about it, not just repeat it.

### The poor image

The **poor image**, a term from Hito Steyerl, is the low-resolution image: compressed, blurred, passed from screen to screen. Steyerl argues it has value of its own, not just as a weak version of a sharp original. What it loses in quality it gains in reach: circulation, the way it moves and spreads, is part of what gives it worth. This is the idea you will actually use in your own work.

### Making an image worse on purpose

In the lab you degrade an image and describe the result precisely. Degrading means to compress it, re-photograph it, or screenshot the screenshot. The task is to say exactly what changed, not simply that it got worse. Naming the change is the skill.
