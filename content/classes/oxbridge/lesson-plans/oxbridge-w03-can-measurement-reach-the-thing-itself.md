## Overview

This lesson asks whether the distance between you and the world comes from your instruments or from being an observer at all. It uses examples you already have, so there is no reading. You build the argument yourself, then commit to a position: is the gap a fact about instruments, or a fact about reality? You leave with one question to answer for next week. Your position and your answer both feed [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w04-is-there-a-reality-behind-appearances|W04]], which opens by polling them.

## The ideas

### Measurement and disturbance

**The gap** ([[concepts/the-gap|The Gap]]) is the distance between an observer and the world being observed. The lesson asks what causes it: your tools, or the act of observing.

**Measurement disturbance** is the way an instrument changes the thing it measures. A thermometer placed in a glass of water changes the temperature of the water. In the double slit experiment, observing which slit a particle passes through changes the result.

You might answer that this is trivial: you calculate the disturbance and subtract it. But to calculate it you need another measurement, which also disturbs. This is a **regress**: the correction never terminates, because each correcting measurement needs correcting too.

You might answer that disturbance only matters at very small scales. Then the question is whether the gap is a fact about reality or only a fact about **magnitude**. And you might answer that better instruments will one day close the gap. That is the position the whole lesson tests.

### The gap in language

Now move the same problem into language. You say the water is cold. I say it is cool. Which of us is wrong? Some words cannot be translated between languages. That may mean the thing they name is harder to reach, not that it does not exist. So which gap is wider: between the word "cold" and the actual temperature, or between the thermometer reading and the actual temperature?

Language is not trying to be precise in the way a measurement is, so it is not failing when it is vague. That can make language better suited to some things than measurement. And **numbers are also a language**: a measurement is a representation too.

The point both examples reach: both language and measurement involve a gap. Science narrows the gap. Nothing closes it. Narrowing is not the same as arriving, and you cannot describe a state with no gap without using a representation.

### From instruments to reality

The final move is to choose: is the gap a fact about our instruments, or a fact about reality? Saying "about reality" is already a step into the **ontological turn** ([[concepts/the-ontological-turn|The Ontological Turn]]), the shift from asking how we know the world to asking what the world is.

## Day by day

- **This week.** In one 45-minute session you work from the thermometer and double slit examples to the language examples, then commit to a position on whether the gap is about instruments or about reality. Homework: bring an answer to the closing question, "A tree falls in a forest with no one there. Does it make a sound?" [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w04-is-there-a-reality-behind-appearances|W04]] opens by polling it.

## Review

Check you can:

- Build an argument from examples you already have, without leaning on a reading.
- Explain **measurement disturbance** using the thermometer and the double slit experiment.
- Explain why correcting for a disturbance leads to a **regress** that does not terminate.
- Compare the gap in language with the gap in measurement, and say why science narrows the gap but nothing closes it.
- Say whether the gap is an instrument problem or a condition of observing, and name what stepping toward "reality" has to do with the **ontological turn**.
- Name what an instrument changes by measuring.
