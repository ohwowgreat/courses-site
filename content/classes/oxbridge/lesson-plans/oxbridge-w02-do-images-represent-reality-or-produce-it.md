## Overview

This lesson asks whether the quality of an image is a technical fact or a political one. You read Steyerl's "In Defense of the Poor Image" and work from two versions of one image, a sharp one and a degraded one, to test what "more real" means. What you produce is a single committed sentence, with one reason, on whether images show us the world or make the world showable. The closing question about a thermometer hands you straight into [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w03-can-measurement-reach-the-thing-itself|W03]].

## The ideas

### The poor image

The **poor image** is Hito Steyerl's term for a low-resolution image that has been copied, compressed, screenshotted, and re-uploaded many times. It is blurry and cheap because it has traveled. The high-resolution image is clean because it was kept and controlled. So resolution is not only physics. It is a marker of value, of institutional access, and of who is permitted to circulate an image. As Steyerl puts it: the poor image is degraded because it moved; the high-resolution image is pristine because it was kept.

You can measure compression, and that stays true. The harder point is why those measurable properties line up so neatly with ownership and access. If an image loses quality every time it is shared, then high quality also tells you how little an image was shared.

Steyerl argues the poor image carries defiance and appropriation as well as conformism and exploitation. It can be both at once. Be careful with the easy version of this: a poor image may be cheaper to distribute without being more democratic. Those are not the same claim.

The idea reaches past digital files. It also covers reproduction in general: prints, copies, textbook plates, museum lighting. A screenshot of a painting raises the same question as a compressed photograph.

### Representing reality or producing it

The second idea is a distinction to hold without collapsing. An image can **represent** an event, showing something that happened. Or it can **produce** the event as a public thing, making it showable and shared in the first place. If an event is never photographed, in what sense did it happen publicly? The task is to keep both possibilities open rather than reduce one to the other.

### Back to neutral perception

W01 asked whether perception can be trained toward neutrality. Now push it: if a neutral image were possible, what would it look like, and who could afford it? Image quality has a cost, so neutrality has a price. This is why [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w01-is-perception-ever-neutral|W01]] matters here.

As a closing aside, a thermometer measures temperature but may also change it. Hold that question. It looks like a different subject in W03. It is not.

## Day by day

- **This session (45 minutes).** Both versions of one image go up, and you write in silence on which is "more real". You work through what "more real" means, then Steyerl's reversal that resolution marks access and circulation, then the distinction between representing an event and producing it as public. You close by committing to one sentence, with one reason, on whether images show the world or make it showable. Before class, read Steyerl, pick the sentence you find most arguable, and bring your two versions of one image.

## Review

Check you can:

- Argue whether the quality of an image is a technical fact or a political one.
- Use Steyerl's **poor image** to make a case an opponent has to answer.
- Explain why resolution marks value, institutional access, and circulation, not only physics.
- Hold the distinction between **representing** reality and **producing** it without collapsing one into the other.
