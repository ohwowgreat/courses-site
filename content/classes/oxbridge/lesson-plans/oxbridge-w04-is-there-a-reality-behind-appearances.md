## Overview

Last week you asked how well we can know things. This week you ask what kinds of things there are to know. You read Plato's Allegory of the Cave and look at a Renaissance perspective construction to test whether there is a reality behind appearances. What you produce is a case to bring to Week 5, where the question becomes whether the gap is a loss, a liberation, or just the condition. Bring a case, not a position.

## The ideas

### From knowing to being

**Epistemology** asks how well we know things. **Ontology** asks what kinds of things there are. Moving from the first question to the second is the **ontological turn** [[concepts/the-ontological-turn|The Ontological Turn]].

Take the question: a tree falls in a forest with no one there, does it make a sound? One answer: the vibration happens, but the sound does not, because sound requires a hearer. That is an ontological position, a claim about what exists. Another answer: it makes a sound, because physics does not need an audience. A third: it depends what you mean by "sound."

That third answer is the point. "Sound" has two definitions here, the vibration in the air and the heard experience. So the disagreement may be an argument about a word, not an argument about the world. Ask which definition is doing more work, and how you would tell the two apart.

### Plato's cave

In [[entities/plato|Plato]]'s **Allegory of the Cave** from *Republic* VII, prisoners see shadows on a wall and mistake them for real things. Ask whether our situation is the same as theirs or different. Knowing you might be in a cave may change your feelings about it, but ask whether it changes your relationship to it.

Consider the perfect circle. You have never seen a perfect circle, only roughly circular things, yet you know what a perfect circle is. Where does that knowledge come from? An empiricist answer says you idealize, you average the imperfect ones. The problem: how do you average toward a perfection you have never encountered?

The prisoner who leaves the cave cannot go back. He may be worse off, not better. This is Plato's own worry, so take it seriously.

### Linear perspective

**Linear perspective** [[concepts/linear-perspective|Linear Perspective]] is a Renaissance method that does not show the world, it shows the world from a point. When a perspective becomes invisible as a perspective, you think you are looking at the world itself, not at one view of it. Ask whether perspective is a discovery about vision or an invention that trained vision. (As an aside, this links to Steyerl's argument from Week 2.)

### Reality behind appearances

The central question: is there a reality behind appearances, and if there is, what would count as evidence of it? The second half is the trap. Any evidence arrives as an appearance.

## Review

Check you can:

- State Plato's Allegory of the Cave precisely enough to argue against it.
- Distinguish an epistemological question about how well we know things from an ontological question about what kinds of things there are.
- Explain what the ontological turn is.
- Explain how linear perspective shows the world from a point, not the world itself.
- Bring a case, not a position, to Week 5.
