# Oxbridge Course Design Principles

> [[classes/oxbridge/oxbridge|Oxbridge]]

Six rules shape each session: how it is built, how questions are worded, and what a good session looks like.

## 1. The question does the work

The teacher does not deliver a position. A question draws one out of you. In a well-run session, you and your classmates do the arguing and the teacher mostly redirects.

## 2. Build from what you already know

Sessions never open with a philosopher. They open with something you can already answer from experience: a map, a thermometer, a feeling of cold, a video-game boundary, a photograph on a phone. The philosopher arrives later, only to **name** what you have already been arguing.

You will see this in every session:

- Berger goes on the board in [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w01-is-perception-ever-neutral|W01]] only after you have generated the idea yourselves.
- Barthes arrives at the *end* of [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w06-does-reality-have-a-history|W06]], to name what you built out of maps and video-game boundaries.
- Spinoza's reframe closes [[classes/oxbridge/lesson-plans/oxbridge-w08-can-the-body-be-trusted|W08]] rather than opening it.

## 3. Questions are plain on the surface, catalytic underneath

The questions are written as single plain lines:

- "What does the world look like?"
- "Where does the body end?"
- "Who agreed that this is real?"

There are no sub-questions or explanations attached to the question itself. The complication comes in the live follow-up, not on the slide. This is why [[classes/oxbridge/oxbridge-question-bank|the question bank]] is written as single lines.

## 4. Close with a defensible position, not a summary

Every session ends by asking you to state a position you are willing to defend under pressure. That closing question is carried out of the room, not resolved in it.

## 5. Complication comes from inside your own claim

The Socratic move is not to bring in a counterexample from outside. It is to find the consequence you have not yet noticed inside your own claim, and to ask about that.

For example:

> "You said the body cannot lie. What about phantom limb pain?" is weaker than "You said the body cannot lie. Does that mean a person who feels pain in a missing leg is feeling something true?"

Both use the same case. The second makes your own claim do the work.

## 6. Register

Sentences are short and vocabulary is plain. **Difficulty lives in the idea, never in the syntax.** A hard concept in easy words is the target. This is why the course can run Foucault, Bergson and Emmelhainz with Grade 11 ESL students without dilution — the concepts are not simplified, the sentences are.

## How the principles show up elsewhere

- The five-part session shape in [[classes/oxbridge/oxbridge-session-structure-template|the template]] makes principles 1 and 4 structural: two minutes of silent writing so you enter with something of your own, and a commit movement where neutrality is the one banned position.
- [[classes/oxbridge/oxbridge-interview-preparation|Interview Preparation]] turns principle 5 into a drill — you perform the push line yourself instead of the teacher.
- The four qualities the course names — commitment, pressure, precision, movement — are principle 4 stated as feedback. Nothing is graded ([[classes/oxbridge/assessments/oxbridge-writing-and-assessment|Assessment — none]]), so these are said aloud in the commit movement rather than written down: "that is a survey, not a position" is a sentence you can act on immediately.
